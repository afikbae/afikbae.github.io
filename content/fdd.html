Frequency domain decomposition

Output-only modal analysis asks what a structure is doing when only its vibration is measured. This page derives frequency domain decomposition from first principles: energy, the equation of motion, eigenvalues, transfer functions, power spectra, and the singular value decomposition. It then derives enhanced FDD for damping. Every approximation is stated where it enters, together with the assumption that licenses it.

Assumptions we use

The derivation is exact under A0–A6 and A8. A7 is an estimation assumption; A9 and A10 are the FDD and EFDD approximations. Each is used where noted and revisited in §9.

Model

A0
Output-only. Only \(y(t)\) is measured; \(x(t)\) is unknown and modeled as random.
A1
Linear, time-invariant, finite-dimensional. \(n\) coordinates, \(r\) inputs, \(p\) sensors; small displacements about equilibrium; \(M,C,K\) constant.
A2
Viscous damping. Constant symmetric \(C\). General damping makes the modes complex; the residue derivation still applies.
A3
Linear input and output maps. Forces enter as \(Bx\), sensors read \(Lq\); no sensor dynamics, delays, or nonlinearities.
A4
Matrix properties. \(M=M^T\succ0\), \(K=K^T\succeq0\), \(C=C^T\succeq0\); \(K\succ0\) if restrained. Derived from energy in §2.4.
A5
Simple, stable eigenvalues. The \(2n\) eigenvalues are distinct, stable, and in conjugate pairs; every pole is simple. Repeated or defective roots are excluded.

Excitation and measurement

A6
Stationary random excitation. \(x\) is zero-mean, wide-sense stationary, with finite variance and a spectral density matrix; ergodicity lets time averages estimate ensemble averages.
A7
Accurate estimate. \(\widehat G_y\) is close to \(G_y\); finite-record errors are treated in Appendix D.
A8
Clean, synchronized measurements. Noise is zero or uncorrelated with the response; channels are synchronized and calibrated.
A9
One dominant mode in the band. \(G_x(f)\approx Q_x\), and the residual \(E_m\) is small relative to the selected term. Made precise in §6.
A10
One mode in the bell. The EFDD bell contains one mode and its correlation decays as a single exponential.

Conventions

A11
Fourier and spectral conventions. \(j^2=-1\); Fourier transform \(e^{-j2\pi ft}\); \(G_y\) two-sided unless marked \(^+\); one-sided spectra double interior bins but not DC or Nyquist; \(f\) in hertz, \(\omega=2\pi f\); displacement PSD in \(\text{unit}^2/\text{Hz}\).
A12
Scale and phase are not identified. The shape is fixed only up to a complex scale; modal mass is not identifiable.

Part IThe model

1The measurement problem

Sensors record the response \(y(t)\); the forces \(x(t)\) are unknown. We want the frequencies at which the structure vibrates, the relative motion of the sensors at those frequencies, and the decay rate. A mode shape is a fixed pattern of relative amplitude and phase: with one mode, every record is a scalar multiple of one vector, so the ratio between two sensors does not change with time. With several modes the ratio varies, and the pattern is visible only in a narrow band around a resonance.

FDD estimates the cross-spectral matrix \(G_y(f)\) and decomposes it at each frequency; the dominant direction at a peak is the shape. EFDD selects a band around a peak, inverse-transforms it, and fits the correlation decay to get damping. Because the inputs are not measured, the shape is determined only up to a complex scale; §6 shows why.

2Energy and the equation of motion

FDD relies on symmetry and positive semidefiniteness, which come from energy. We derive the equation of motion from energy and check it with Lagrange’s equations.

2.1Energy of a vibrating structure

Let \(q(t)\in\mathbb R^n\) collect displacements from static equilibrium. For a linear structure,

\[T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q,\qquad V=\tfrac12q^TKq,\qquad D=\tfrac12\dot q^TC\dot q.\]
(2.1)

\(M,K,C\) are the mass, stiffness, and viscous damping matrices. External forces do virtual work

\[\delta W=(Bx)^T\delta q=x^TB^T\delta q,\]

where \(B\) is \(n\times r\); \(Bx\) is the generalized force. Sensors read

\[y=Lq,\]

with \(L\) of size \(p\times n\). The quadratic forms follow from a Taylor expansion about equilibrium: the linear term vanishes at a stationary point, the constant can be taken as zero, and the quadratic term dominates for small displacements (A1). The matrices are second derivatives of the energy, hence symmetric.

2.2One degree of freedom

Take a mass \(\mu>0\), spring \(k>0\), damper \(b\ge0\), force \(F(t)\), and displacement \(u(t)\). The energies and virtual work are

\[T=\tfrac12\mu\dot u^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12ku^2,\qquad D=\tfrac12b\dot u^2,\qquad \delta W=F\,\delta u.\]
(2.2)

Energy balance equates the rate of stored energy to the supplied power minus the dissipated power:

\[\frac{d}{dt}(T+V)=F\dot u-b\dot u^2.\]
(2.3)

Differentiate and factor:

\[\mu\dot u\ddot u+ku\dot u=F\dot u-b\dot u^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\dot u\bigl(\mu\ddot u+b\dot u+ku-F\bigr)=0.\]

When \(\dot u\ne0\), divide. When \(\dot u=0\), continuity of \(\ddot u\) and \(F\) makes the bracket vanish as a limit. Hence

\[\mu\ddot u+b\dot u+ku=F.\]
(2.4)

Each term is a force. Dividing by \(\mu\) gives the normalized form

\[\ddot u+\frac{b}{\mu}\dot u+\frac{k}{\mu}u=\frac{F}{\mu}.\]

Lagrange’s equations give the same result and generalize to matrices. With Lagrangian \(T-V\), dissipation \(D\), and generalized force \(Q\),

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}-\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot u}=Q.\]
(2.5)

Here \(T-V=\tfrac12\mu\dot u^2-\tfrac12ku^2\), \(D=\tfrac12b\dot u^2\), and \(Q=F\). The derivatives are

\[\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}=\mu\dot u,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}=\mu\ddot u,\qquad \frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial u}=-ku,\qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot u}=b\dot u.\]

Substitution returns (2.4).

2.3Many degrees of freedom

For the full structure, energy balance is

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q+\tfrac12q^TKq\right)=\dot q^TBx-\dot q^TC\dot q.\]
(2.6)

With \(M,K\) constant and symmetric,

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\right)=\dot q^TM\ddot q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12q^TKq\right)=\dot q^TKq.\]

Collecting terms gives

\[\dot q^T\bigl(M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq-Bx\bigr)=0.\]

This holds for every \(\dot q\). Taking \(\dot q=e_i\) for each \(i\) makes every component of the bracket zero, so

\[M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=Bx,\qquad y=Lq.\]
(2.7)

2.4What the matrices mean

Symmetry. \(q^TAq=q^TA^Tq\), so only the symmetric part contributes; take \(M,K,C\) symmetric. For \(M,K\) this follows from energy; for \(C\) it is A2.

Definiteness. \(A\) is positive semidefinite if \(x^TAx\ge0\), positive definite if strict for \(x\ne0\). \(T\ge0\), with equality only at \(\dot q=0\), so \(M\succ0\) and invertible. \(V\ge0\); a restrained structure has \(K\succ0\), a free-free structure has \(K\succeq0\) with rigid-body \(K\phi=0\). \(D\ge0\), so \(C\succeq0\), possibly singular.

Units. \(q\) in metres and \(x\) in newtons give \(M\) in kilograms, \(C\) in newton-seconds per metre, \(K\) in newtons per metre; each term of (2.7) is a force. \(B\) absorbs input units and \(L\) sensor units.

Two masses. Masses \(m_1,m_2\) are joined by springs \(k_1,k_2,k_3\) and dampers \(c_1,c_2,c_3\), with \(k_1,c_1\) from the wall to \(m_1\), \(k_2,c_2\) between the masses, and \(k_3,c_3\) from \(m_2\) to the wall. With \(q=[q_1,q_2]^T\),

\[M=\begin{bmatrix}m_1&0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}k_1+k_2&-k_2\\-k_2&k_2+k_3\end{bmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{bmatrix}c_1+c_2&-c_2\\-c_2&c_2+c_3\end{bmatrix}.\]
(2.8)

The off-diagonal entries are negative because the middle spring and damper pull the masses together. The energies are

\[V=\tfrac12\left[k_1q_1^2+k_2(q_2-q_1)^2+k_3q_2^2\right],\qquad D=\tfrac12\left[c_1\dot q_1^2+c_2(\dot q_2-\dot q_1)^2+c_3\dot q_2^2\right].\]

Each bracket is a sum of squares with positive coefficients, so \(V,D\ge0\). \(K\succ0\) when \(k_1+k_2>0\) and \(k_1k_2+k_1k_3+k_2k_3>0\), which holds for positive springs. A force on \(m_1\) gives \(B=[1,0]^T\); measuring both displacements gives \(L=I\).

2.5Lagrange’s equations for the matrix case

With \(q\) as the generalized coordinate and \(Q=Bx\),

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot q}=Q.\]
(2.9)

Since \(T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\) and \(V\) does not depend on \(\dot q\),

\[\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}=M\dot q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}=M\ddot q,\]

because \(M\) is constant. Since \(V=\tfrac12q^TKq\), component \(i\) of its gradient is

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i}=\frac12\sum_{j,k}K_{jk}\frac{\partial(q_jq_k)}{\partial q_i}=\frac12\sum_kK_{ik}q_k+\frac12\sum_jK_{ji}q_j=(Kq)_i,\]

using \(K_{ji}=K_{ij}\) and renaming the index. Also \(\partial D/\partial\dot q=C\dot q\). Substituting into (2.9) with \(\partial(T-V)/\partial q=-\partial V/\partial q\) gives

\[M\ddot q+Kq+C\dot q=Bx,\]

which is (2.7). Lagrange’s form holds for any generalized coordinates and allows \(M\) to be non-diagonal.

3Modal analysis: free vibration

Free vibration is the motion with no force. Its solutions are the modes: fixed spatial patterns that evolve at a single complex rate.

3.1The exponential ansatz and the quadratic eigenproblem

Set \(x=0\) in (2.7):

\[M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=0.\]

This linear constant-coefficient equation has exponential solutions; polynomial factors occur only for repeated eigenvalues, excluded by A5. Seek

\[q_c(t)=\phi e^{\lambda t},\qquad \dot q_c=\lambda\phi e^{\lambda t},\qquad \ddot q_c=\lambda^2\phi e^{\lambda t}.\]
(3.1)

Here \(c\) marks the complex auxiliary solution, \(\phi\ne0\) is the spatial pattern, and \(\lambda\) is the complex rate. Substitution gives

\[\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda C+K\right)\phi\,e^{\lambda t}=0.\]

Cancel the exponential and define

\[Z(s)=s^2M+sC+K,\qquad Z(\lambda)\phi=0,\qquad \det Z(\lambda)=0.\]
(3.2)

Nonzero \(\phi\) makes \(Z(\lambda)\) singular; a zero determinant gives a null vector. The scale of \(\phi\) is arbitrary; only its direction matters. This is a quadratic eigenvalue problem. \(\det Z(s)\) has degree \(2n\) because its leading term is \(\det(M)s^{2n}\), so there are \(2n\) roots, and real matrices give conjugate pairs. A numerical solver uses the \(2n\times2n\) state form \(z=[q;\dot q]\), whose eigenvectors stack \(\phi_m\) on \(\lambda_m\phi_m\) (Appendix A).

3.2Real motion from complex modes

\(q_c\) is complex, but the physical \(q\) is real. Real matrices give \(\bar\lambda\) and \(\bar\phi\); combine the pair:

\[q_m(t)=c\,\phi e^{\lambda t}+\bar c\,\bar\phi e^{\bar\lambda t}=2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(c\,\phi e^{\lambda t}\right).\]
(3.3)

by \(z+\bar z=2\operatorname{Re}z\). Each coordinate is real, and the \(2n\) real constants in the \(n\) coefficients \(c_m\) match the initial conditions. Write \(\lambda=\sigma+j\omega_d\):

\[\lambda=\sigma+j\omega_d,\qquad e^{\lambda t}=e^{\sigma t}\left[\cos(\omega_d t)+j\sin(\omega_d t)\right].\]
(3.4)

Stability means \(\sigma<0\). Choose \(\omega_d>0\) and write \(\lambda=-\alpha+j\omega_d\) with \(\alpha>0\): \(\alpha\) is the decay rate, \(\omega_d\) the damped angular frequency. For \(C=0\) or proportional damping, \(\phi\) can be real; for general \(C\) it is complex and records relative phases, not a complex displacement.

3.3The equivalent single-degree-of-freedom oscillator

Natural frequency and damping ratio come from a scalar oscillator. With mass \(\mu>0\), spring \(k>0\), damper \(b\ge0\), set \(F=0\) in (2.4) and divide by \(\mu\):

\[\ddot u+\frac{b}{\mu}\dot u+\frac{k}{\mu}u=0.\]
(3.5)

With \(b=0\), \(\ddot u+(k/\mu)u=0\), so \(\omega_n^2=k/\mu\) and \(f_n=\omega_n/(2\pi)\). Restoring the damper and substituting \(u=e^{st}\):

\[\mu s^2+bs+k=0,\qquad s=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4\mu k}}{2\mu}.\]
(3.6)

The discriminant decides: \(b^2<4\mu k\) gives complex roots and oscillation, \(b^2>4\mu k\) gives real negative roots and no oscillation, and equality gives a double root. The boundary is \(b_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{\mu k}\), so

\[b_{\mathrm{crit}}^2-4\mu k=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{\mu k},\qquad \zeta\equiv\frac{b}{b_{\mathrm{crit}}}=\frac{b}{2\sqrt{\mu k}}.\]

The dimensionless \(\zeta\) is \(<1\) underdamped, \(=1\) critical, \(>1\) overdamped; the factor 2 comes from (3.6). Since \(b/\mu=2\zeta\omega_n\), dividing (2.4) by \(\mu\) gives

\[\ddot u+2\zeta\omega_n\dot u+\omega_n^2u=0,\qquad s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=0.\]

For \(\zeta<1\), \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). Comparing with \(\lambda=-\alpha\pm j\omega_d\) gives \(\alpha=\zeta\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). Matching the full polynomial,

\[(s-\lambda)(s-\bar\lambda)=(s+\alpha)^2+\omega_d^2=s^2+2\alpha s+(\alpha^2+\omega_d^2),\]

so \(\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}=\alpha_m\), \(\omega_{n,m}^2=\alpha_m^2+\omega_{d,m}^2\), and

\[\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|=\sqrt{\alpha_m^2+\omega_{d,m}^2},\qquad \zeta_m=\frac{\alpha_m}{\omega_{n,m}},\qquad \omega_{d,m}=\omega_{n,m}\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}.\]
(3.7)

These are the modal parameters. \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\) is the equivalent-oscillator parameter, not necessarily an undamped structural frequency (§3.5).

3.4Interactive: damping regimes

Release the oscillator from \(u(0)=1\), \(\dot u(0)=0\). For \(\zeta<1\), \(u=e^{-\alpha t}(C_1\cos\omega_dt+C_2\sin\omega_dt)\); the conditions give \(C_1=1\) and \(C_2=\alpha/\omega_d\). For \(\zeta=1\), \(u=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\omega_nt}\) gives \(C_1=1\), \(C_2=\omega_n\). For \(\zeta>1\), \(\beta=\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\), \(\lambda_{1,2}=-\alpha\pm\beta\), and the two conditions give the coefficients below:

\[u(t)=\begin{cases}e^{-\alpha t}\left[\cos(\omega_dt)+\dfrac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt)\right],&0\le\zeta<1,\\(1+\omega_nt)e^{-\omega_nt},&\zeta=1,\\\dfrac{-\lambda_2e^{\lambda_1t}+\lambda_1e^{\lambda_2t}}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2},&\zeta>1.\end{cases}\]
(3.8)

The critical case is the limit of the underdamped case; the overdamped case reduces to it as \(\beta\to0\). The figure shows the poles and the response.

3.5Two meanings of natural frequency

“Remove the damping” has two meanings. For the equivalent oscillator, set its \(\zeta\) to zero while keeping \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\). For the structure, set \(C=0\) in (2.7) and solve

\[M\ddot q+Kq=0,\qquad K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r.\]

For one mass and spring both give \(\sqrt{k/\mu}\). Under proportional damping \(C=aM+bK\), the damped modes are the undamped modes with an added decay; Appendix A shows \(\omega_{n,r}=\Omega_r\) and \(\zeta_r=(a/\Omega_r+b\Omega_r)/2\). For general damping, modes can mix undamped shapes and \(|\lambda_m|\) need not equal any \(\Omega_r\). Here \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\).

The next part connects these modes to forced measurements through the transfer matrix.

Part IIFrom forces to spectra

4The transfer matrix and its residues

Free vibration gives the poles; the transfer matrix gives their residues. It maps forces to measurements in the frequency domain.

4.1The Laplace transform

For a signal \(q(t)\) on \(t\ge0\),

\[Q(s)=\mathcal L\{q\}(s)=\int_0^\infty q(t)e^{-st}\,dt,\qquad s=\sigma+j\omega.\]
(4.1)

This is a Fourier transform of \(q(t)e^{-\sigma t}\); choosing \(\sigma\) large enough makes it converge. The derivative rule follows from integration by parts with \(u=e^{-st}\), \(dv=\dot q\,dt\):

\[\int_0^\infty\dot q(t)e^{-st}\,dt=\bigl[q(t)e^{-st}\bigr]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty q(t)e^{-st}\,dt=sQ(s)-q(0).\]

The upper limit vanishes by the choice of \(\sigma\); the lower limit gives \(-q(0)\). Applying the rule again,

\[\mathcal L\{\dot q\}(s)=sQ(s)-q(0),\qquad \mathcal L\{\ddot q\}(s)=s^2Q(s)-sq(0)-\dot q(0).\]
(4.2)

With zero initial conditions each derivative is multiplication by \(s\); the initial-condition response can be added separately. On the imaginary axis, \(s=j\omega=j2\pi f\), this is the stationary frequency response.

4.2From the equation of motion to \(H(s)\)

Transform (2.7) with zero initial conditions:

\[\underbrace{(s^2M+sC+K)}_{Z(s)}Q(s)=BX(s),\qquad Y(s)=LQ(s).\]
(4.3)

Where \(Z(s)\) is invertible,

\[Q(s)=Z(s)^{-1}BX(s),\qquad Y(s)=LZ(s)^{-1}BX(s),\]

so

\[Y(s)=H(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=LZ(s)^{-1}B.\]
(4.4)

\(H\) is \(p\times r\); \(H_{ik}\) maps input \(k\) to sensor \(i\). Its entries share the denominator \(\det Z(s)\). \(Z\) is singular exactly at the eigenvalues, so the poles of \(H\) are eigenvalues unless cancelled by \(L\) or \(B\).

4.3Partial fractions at a simple pole

For one entry with a simple pole at \(\lambda_m\),

\[H_{ik}(s)=\frac{r_{ik,m}}{s-\lambda_m}+g_{ik,m}(s),\]
(4.5)

where \(g_{ik,m}\) is finite at \(\lambda_m\). This is exact. Multiply by \(s-\lambda_m\) and take the limit:

\[\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H_{ik}(s)=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}\left[r_{ik,m}+(s-\lambda_m)g_{ik,m}(s)\right]=r_{ik,m}.\]
(4.6)

The second term vanishes, so the limit isolates the residue. Entrywise this gives a matrix \(R_m\) and a remainder:

\[H(s)=\frac{R_m}{s-\lambda_m}+H_{\mathrm{rest},m}(s),\qquad R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s).\]

\(R_m\) is \(p\times r\); \(H_{\mathrm{rest},m}\) contains the conjugate pole and all other modes. A simple pole (A5) makes the principal part one term.

4.4Why the residue is rank one

Separate the force-to-coordinate transfer from the sensor map:

\[\mathcal T(s)=Z(s)^{-1}B,\qquad Q(s)=\mathcal T(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=L\mathcal T(s).\]

Let \(W_m\) be the residue of \(\mathcal T\) at \(\lambda_m\):

\[W_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)\mathcal T(s).\]

Multiply \(Z(s)\mathcal T(s)=B\) by \(s-\lambda_m\) and take the limit:

\[Z(\lambda_m)W_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)B=0.\]
(4.7)

Each column of \(W_m\) lies in the nullspace of \(Z(\lambda_m)\), the set of vectors mapped to zero. At a simple eigenvalue that nullspace is one-dimensional, so every column is a multiple of \(\phi_m\). If input \(k\) has scalar \(\gamma_{m,k}\),

\[W_m=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\gamma_{m,1}&\cdots&\phi_m\gamma_{m,r}\end{bmatrix}=\phi_m\gamma_m^T,\qquad \gamma_m^T=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{m,1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m,r}\end{bmatrix}.\]

Apply \(L\), which is constant and passes through the limit:

\[R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s)=L\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)\mathcal T(s)=LW_m=(L\phi_m)\gamma_m^T=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(4.8)

The residue is rank one: every column is a multiple of the measured shape \(v_m\). If \(v_m=0\) the sensors do not see the mode; if \(\gamma_m=0\) the inputs do not excite it.

For two inputs with coefficients \(2\) and \(-3\), and \(v_m=[1,-2]^T\),

\[R_m=\begin{bmatrix}2v_m&-3v_m\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-3\\-4&6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&-3\end{bmatrix}.\]

Both columns keep the ratio \(1:-2\); an input vector combines them into \(v_m(2X_1-3X_2)\). The full \(H\) need not be rank one, because it contains the other modes. The rank-one statement is about one residue.

4.5The full modal sum

For real matrices the conjugate pole has residue \(\bar R_m\). Summing the \(n\) pairs,

\[H(s)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\frac{R_m}{s-\lambda_m}+\frac{\bar R_m}{s-\bar\lambda_m}\right),\qquad R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(4.9)

This is exact under A5, not a one-mode assumption. A zero \(v_m\), zero \(\gamma_m\), or cancellation removes a term from \(H\); the mode still exists.

5The response spectral matrix

Operational forces are unknown and vary between records. We average products of Fourier coefficients, not the response itself; that average is the spectral matrix.

5.1From records to a spectral matrix

Let \(T\) be the record duration and \(y(t)\) the \(p\)-channel measurement. Define

\[Y_T(f)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}y(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt,\qquad G_y(f)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb E\!\left[Y_T(f)Y_T(f)^H\right].\]
(5.1)

\(Y_T\) is random; \(\mathbb E\) averages over records. The \((i,k)\) entry of \(Y_TY_T^H\) is \(Y_i\bar Y_k\). The factor \(1/T\) converts squared transform (amplitude squared times time squared) to a density (amplitude squared per hertz). Integrating a diagonal entry over all frequencies gives that channel’s mean square, equal to its variance because the mean is zero.

The matrix is two-sided. For real signals the negative frequencies mirror the positive ones; a one-sided spectrum, marked \(^+\), doubles interior positive bins and keeps DC and Nyquist unchanged. The definition assumes wide-sense stationarity (A6): expected products depend only on time separation, so the limit exists. Ergodicity lets a time average estimate the ensemble average; estimation details are in Appendix D.

5.2Hermitian positive semidefiniteness

For any \(Y\),

\[\left(YY^H\right)^H=\left(Y^H\right)^HY^H=YY^H.\]

A matrix equal to its conjugate transpose is Hermitian; expectation preserves this, so \(G_y\) is Hermitian. Its diagonal entries are averages of \(|Y_i|^2\), hence real and nonnegative. For any complex weight vector \(a\), \(|a^HY_T|^2\ge0\), and averaging gives

\[G_y^H=G_y,\qquad a^HG_ya=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb E\!\left[|a^HY_T|^2\right]\ge0.\]
(5.2)

So \(G_y\) is Hermitian positive semidefinite. The spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis and real eigenvalues; positive semidefiniteness makes them nonnegative. With unitary \(U\) and diagonal \(\Lambda\),

\[G_y=U\Lambda U^H,\qquad U^HU=I,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p),\quad \lambda_k\ge0.\]

For such a matrix the singular values are the eigenvalues and the left singular vectors are the eigenvectors. The rank is the number of nonzero eigenvalues; a rank-one matrix has one eigenvector and a one-dimensional nullspace.

5.3The input–output relation

At a fixed frequency, the stationary output transform is the transfer matrix on the imaginary axis times the input transform:

\[Y_T(f)=H(j2\pi f)X_T(f).\]

This is (4.4) at \(s=j2\pi f\), for the forced part after the transient has decayed. Substitute into the outer product:

\[Y_TY_T^H=\left(HX_T\right)\left(HX_T\right)^H=HX_TX_T^HH^H.\]

At fixed frequency \(H\) is constant across realizations, so it passes through the expectation. Applying (5.1),

\[G_y(f)=H(j2\pi f)\,G_x(f)\,H(j2\pi f)^H.\]
(5.3)

\(G_x\) is \(r\times r\) and Hermitian positive semidefinite; \(G_y\) is \(p\times p\). The force spectrum describes the excitation, \(H\) the response and the sensors.

5.4Conventions and estimation

This page defines entry \((i,k)\) as \(Y_i\bar Y_k\). Some software uses \(\bar Y_iY_k\), the transpose and conjugate; the estimated shape is then conjugated. Do not mix conventions. A one-sided spectrum keeps \(f\ge0\) and doubles interior bins so that integrating over \(f\ge0\) matches the two-sided mean square; DC and Nyquist are not doubled. The Welch estimator, its normalization, and the resolution–variance tradeoff are in Appendix D.

Part IIIFinding modes in data

6When one mode dominates

The response matrix is an exact sum of rank-one terms. FDD uses the band where one term dominates and the others are a perturbation. This section isolates that term and bounds the remainder.

6.1Isolating one pole

On the imaginary axis, \(s=j\omega\) with \(\omega=2\pi f\), separate mode \(m\):

\[H(j\omega)=\frac{R_m}{j\omega-\lambda_m}+E_m(j\omega),\qquad R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(6.1)

\(E_m\) contains the conjugate pole and every other mode and is finite at \(\omega_{d,m}\) (A5). The selected denominator is

\[j\omega-\lambda_m=\alpha_m+j(\omega-\omega_{d,m}),\qquad |j\omega-\lambda_m|^2=\alpha_m^2+(\omega-\omega_{d,m})^2.\]

At \(\omega=\omega_{d,m}\) its magnitude is \(\alpha_m\), so the selected term grows like \(1/\alpha_m\). The remainder has no such denominator.

6.2The exact four-term expansion

With \(P=R_m/(j\omega-\lambda_m)\) and \(E=E_m\), substitute into (5.3):

\[G_y=(P+E)G_x(P+E)^H=PG_xP^H+PG_xE^H+EG_xP^H+EG_xE^H.\]
(6.2)

This is exact. The first term has the selected pole twice; the middle terms mix it with the remainder; the last has the remainder twice. Since \(P^H=R_m^H/(-j\omega-\bar\lambda_m)\),

\[PG_xP^H=\frac{R_mG_xR_m^H}{|j\omega-\lambda_m|^2}.\]

With \(R_m^H=\bar\gamma_mv_m^H\),

\[R_mG_xR_m^H=v_m\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m\,v_m^H=\left(\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m\right)v_mv_m^H.\]

Define the scalar weight

\[G_y\approx d_m\,v_mv_m^H,\qquad d_m(f)=\frac{\gamma_m^TG_x(f)\bar\gamma_m}{\alpha_m^2+(2\pi f-\omega_{d,m})^2}\ge0.\]
(6.3)

The numerator is \((\bar\gamma_m)^HG_x(\bar\gamma_m)\ge0\) because \(G_x\) is positive semidefinite. The direction \(v_m\) is fixed; only \(d_m\) varies with frequency. For constant \(G_x\approx Q_x\),

\[d_m(f)=\frac{\gamma_m^TQ_x\bar\gamma_m}{\alpha_m^2+(2\pi f-\omega_{d,m})^2},\]

a Lorentzian peaked at \(\omega_{d,m}\), with height set by the numerator and width by \(\alpha_m\).

6.3The residual bound

Dropping the last three terms of (6.2) is legitimate when they are small. Weight selected term and remainder by the excitation; let \(G_x^{1/2}\) be the PSD square root and

\[P_w=PG_x^{1/2},\qquad E_w=EG_x^{1/2}.\]

Then \(P_wP_w^H=PG_xP^H\), \(P_wE_w^H=PG_xE^H\), \(E_wP_w^H=EG_xP^H\), \(E_wE_w^H=EG_xE^H\), so

\[G_y=P_wP_w^H+P_wE_w^H+E_wP_w^H+E_wE_w^H.\]

With \(\|\cdot\|_2\) the spectral norm, the triangle inequality and \(\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2\) give

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le2\|P_w\|_2\|E_w\|_2+\|E_w\|_2^2.\]
(6.4)

If \(\|E_w\|_2\le\varepsilon\|P_w\|_2\), then

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)\|P_w\|_2^2.\]

\(\varepsilon\) depends on the force spectrum, modal visibility, and pole separation, not on damping alone. A lightly damped mode can have large \(\varepsilon\) near a strongly excited mode; a heavily damped mode can have small \(\varepsilon\) if it is alone in the band. Appendix C repeats the bound.

6.4What the assumption really says

It does not say the structure has one mode, or that damping is small. It says that in this band the selected mode dominates the response that is actually excited and observed. A mode can be invisible because \(v_m\) is small (sensors near a node) or \(\gamma_m\) is small (forces do not excite it), even if it is lightly damped. Close modes make \(E\) large; colored forcing distorts the weight and can shift the peak; correlated inputs matter when several modes contribute; noise adds a full-rank term.

6.5Worked example: two sensors

Suppose one mode moves sensor 1 by \(1\) mm and sensor 2 by \(-2\) mm. With \(a(t)\) the first displacement,

\[y(t)=\begin{bmatrix}a(t)\\-2a(t)\end{bmatrix}=v\,a(t),\qquad v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}.\]
(6.5)

Let \(A(f)\) be the Fourier coefficient of \(a\). Linearity gives \(Y_1=A\), \(Y_2=-2A\), so

\[|Y_1|^2=|A|^2,\qquad |Y_2|^2=4|A|^2,\qquad Y_1\bar Y_2=-2|A|^2.\]

The separate power spectra lose the sign; the cross product preserves the opposite motion. Averaging and normalizing,

\[G_y(f)=S_a(f)\,vv^T=S_a(f)\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\-2&4\end{bmatrix},\]
(6.6)

where \(S_a\) is the PSD of \(a\). For \(S_a>0\) the matrix is rank one, and

\[G_yv=5S_a(f)\,v,\qquad G_y\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=0.\]
(6.7)

The eigenvalue depends on excitation strength; the eigenvector gives \(1:-2\) regardless. The null vector \([2,1]^T\) is orthogonal to \(v\). The shape is defined only up to scale and phase, and where \(S_a=0\) there is no preferred direction. FDD therefore estimates shapes at peaks.

7Frequency domain decomposition

FDD diagonalizes the measured spectral matrix at each frequency. The decomposition is exact; reading the leading vector as a mode shape uses the approximation of §6.

7.1The SVD at each frequency

At frequency \(f_i\), diagonalize the estimate \(\widehat G_y\):

\[\widehat G_y(f_i)=U_iS_iU_i^H=\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_k(f_i)\,u_k(f_i)u_k(f_i)^H,\qquad s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge s_p\ge0.\]
(7.1)

The columns of \(U_i\) are orthonormal eigenvectors; \(S_i\) is diagonal. For a Hermitian positive-semidefinite matrix the SVD and eigendecomposition coincide: the singular values are the eigenvalues, and the left singular vectors are the eigenvectors. The ordering makes the first term largest.

An exact rank-one matrix has only \(s_1\ne0\). The estimate is generally full rank because of other modes, noise, and finite-record errors. The question is whether \(s_2\) is small enough that \(u_1\) is close to the physical shape (Appendix E).

7.2The leading singular vector

For \(G\approx d\,vv^H\) with \(v\ne0\),

\[\left(d\,vv^H\right)v=d\,\|v\|^2\,v,\]
(7.2)

so \(v\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(d\|v\|^2\). Normalizing,

\[u_1\approx e^{j\theta}\frac{v}{\|v\|},\qquad s_1\approx d\,\|v\|^2.\]
(7.3)

The phase \(\theta\) is arbitrary, and multiplying \(v\) by a constant while adjusting \(d\) leaves \(d\,vv^H\) unchanged. Neither phase nor scale is physical; this is why modal mass is not identifiable. For \(G=G_0+E\) with \(G_0=d\,vv^H\), the leading vector is close to \(v/\|v\|\) when \(\|E\|_2\) is small relative to the spectral gap; the bound is in Appendix E. A large \(s_1/s_2\) supports a well-defined direction; a ratio near one does not. For the two-sensor example, \(u_1=[1,-2]^T/\sqrt5\) and \(s_1=5S_a\).

7.3Peak frequency is not pole frequency

Even a single oscillator distinguishes the spectral peak from \(\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d\). With subscripts omitted, the force-to-displacement transfer function is

\[h(j\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+j2\zeta\omega_n\omega},\]

so

\[\left|h(j\omega)\right|^2=\frac{1}{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+4\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega^2}.\]
(7.4)

Under white forcing the displacement PSD has the same frequency dependence. Minimize the denominator \(D(\omega)\):

\[D'(\omega)=2(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)(-2\omega)+8\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega=4\omega\left[\omega^2-\omega_n^2+2\zeta^2\omega_n^2\right].\]

The stationary points are \(\omega=0\) and \(\omega^2=\omega_n^2(1-2\zeta^2)\). For \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2\) the positive root is a minimum of \(D\), hence a maximum of \(|h|^2\); for \(\zeta\ge1/\sqrt2\) the maximum is at \(\omega=0\). Therefore

\[\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}\quad(\zeta<1/\sqrt2),\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=0\quad(\zeta\ge1/\sqrt2).\]
(7.5)

For light damping the three frequencies are close; for \(0<\zeta<1/\sqrt2\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}<\omega_d<\omega_n\). Colored forcing, acceleration measurement, and finite resolution shift the observable peak; an acceleration PSD carries an extra \(\omega^4\), biasing it upward.

7.4Two modes in one band

For two modes,

\[G_y=d_1v_1v_1^H+d_2v_2v_2^H.\]
(7.6)

If \(v_1^Hv_2=0\), the eigenvectors are exactly \(v_1,v_2\), with eigenvalues \(d_1\|v_1\|^2,d_2\|v_2\|^2\). Otherwise

\[G_yv_1=d_1\|v_1\|^2v_1+d_2v_2\left(v_2^Hv_1\right),\]
(7.7)

and the second term points away from \(v_1\), so \(v_1\) is not an eigenvector. The leading vector is a mixture. What survives is the span: if \(d_1,d_2>0\) and \(v_1,v_2\) are independent, \(G_y\) maps into their two-dimensional span, and the first two singular vectors recover that span but not the individual shapes. Orthogonal measured shapes are special; they are not the same as mass-orthogonal physical modes, because \(L\) does not preserve the mass inner product. Equal singular values leave the basis within the subspace undetermined.

The modal assurance criterion measures agreement:

\[\operatorname{MAC}(u,v)=\frac{|u^Hv|^2}{(u^Hu)(v^Hv)}\in[0,1].\]
(7.8)

By Cauchy–Schwarz it lies in \([0,1]\); it is one for proportional vectors, zero for orthogonal vectors, and invariant to a common complex scale. MAC decides whether the direction at a neighboring frequency is the same mode.

7.5The algorithm

FDD, step by step

  1. Acquire. Record \(p\) synchronized channels with known units and orientations; choose segment length, window, and overlap.
  2. Estimate. Compute \(\widehat G_y(f_i)\) for every bin, using the same segments and window for all channel pairs and enough independent segments.
  3. Decompose. Diagonalize at each \(f_i\) (7.1); store the singular values and leading vectors.
  4. Find peaks. Plot \(s_1(f)\); a local maximum is a candidate resonance.
  5. Read the shape. At a peak take \(u_1\); check that \(s_1/s_2\) is large and \(u_1\) is stable across bins by MAC.
  6. Estimate damping. If needed, select the bell around the peak by MAC and fit its correlation decay (§8).

Output. Damped frequency \(f_{d,m}\), shape \(u_1\) up to scale and phase, and, with EFDD, \(\zeta_m\).

The eigendecomposition costs \(O(N_fp^3)\) for \(N_f\) bins and \(p\) channels, small when \(p\) is small. The spectral estimate costs \(O(N\log N)\) per segment. In practice the hard part is sensor placement and spectral settings, not the linear algebra.

8Estimating damping: EFDD

FDD gives shape and frequency. Damping comes from the width of the spectral peak: select the part belonging to one mode, inverse-transform it, and fit the decay of its correlation.

8.1Selecting the spectral bell

Take the leading vector near a peak as the reference \(\hat v_m\). At each neighboring frequency, compute \(\operatorname{MAC}(u_k(f),\hat v_m)\) and keep the bin if it exceeds a threshold; the kept set is the spectral bell. A threshold of \(0.8\) is a common starting point: high enough to reject a neighboring mode, low enough to keep the flanks. Track the direction by MAC, not by vector index, because the first and second singular vectors can cross. Zero the rejected bins and inspect the bell: an asymmetric or rough bell indicates contamination.

The selected spectrum is one-sided, \(b^+(f)\), with \(b^+[k]\) at \(k\Delta f\). The \(^+\) scaling is defined in Appendix D: interior bins are doubled, DC and Nyquist are not.

8.2From the bell to a correlation decay

By Wiener–Khinchin, the inverse transform of the PSD is the autocorrelation. For a real even correlation the result is a cosine integral:

\[\mathcal R_m(\tau)=\int_0^\infty b^+(f)\cos(2\pi f\tau)\,df\approx\Delta f\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}b^+[k]\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k\ell}{N}\right),\qquad \tau=\frac{\ell}{f_s}.\]
(8.1)

Here \(f_s\) is the sampling rate, \(N\) an even transform length, \(\Delta f=f_s/N\), and \(\ell\) the lag index. The cosine comes from mirroring the negative frequencies. For a single mode, the correlation oscillates with envelope \(a_m(\tau)=A_0e^{-\alpha_m\tau}\), \(A_0>0\), and period \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_{d,m}\):

\[\mathcal R_m(\tau)\propto a_m(\tau)\cos(\omega_{d,m}\tau),\qquad T_d=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{d,m}}.\]

8.3From log decrement to damping ratio

At two times separated by \(k\) periods, the envelope ratio is \(e^{-\alpha_mkT_d}\). Its logarithm per period is the logarithmic decrement:

\[\delta=\frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{a_m(\tau)}{a_m(\tau+kT_d)}=\alpha_mT_d.\]
(8.2)

Substitute \(\alpha_m=\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}\) and \(T_d=2\pi/(\omega_{n,m}\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2})\); the natural frequency cancels:

\[\delta=\frac{2\pi\zeta_m}{\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}}.\]

Square and solve for \(\zeta_m\):

\[\delta^2(1-\zeta_m^2)=4\pi^2\zeta_m^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\zeta_m^2=\frac{\delta^2}{4\pi^2+\delta^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\zeta_m=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}},\]

taking the positive root. Then

\[\zeta_m=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}},\qquad f_{n,m}=\frac{f_{d,m}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}}.\]
(8.3)

Fit several peaks: \(\ln a_m(\tau_j)=\ln A_0-\alpha_m\tau_j\) is a line; its slope gives \(\alpha_m\). Same-sign peaks are one period apart, alternating extrema half a period. Exclude the initial distorted part and the late noisy part.

8.4Practical bias

A nearby mode produces beating and biases a single-exponential fit. A bell that is too narrow truncates the peak; one that is too wide admits neighbors and noise. Leakage changes the bell shape, and nonstationary excitation changes its height. Compute the damping for several bell widths and fitting intervals. If the result moves, the bell is not isolating one mode; compare the EFDD frequency with (3.7) before reporting it.

Part IVPractice

9Using the method on measurements

The calculation is short; the checks decide whether the result means anything.

9.1Before the measurement

  • Sensor placement. A sensor at a node records none of that mode. Place sensors where the target modes are large, and use enough to distinguish the shapes; \(v_m=L\phi_m\) is limited by \(L\).
  • Units and directions. Record the measured quantity, units, and positive direction. Arbitrary rescaling changes the geometry seen by the SVD; apply rotations before the spectral estimate.
  • Synchronization. All channels need one time base. A constant offset adds a frequency-dependent phase shift and corrupts the cross-spectra.
  • Stationarity. The excitation must be stationary over the record or each segment; transients, gusts, and operating changes violate A6.

9.2During spectral estimation

  • Same segments and window. Use them for every entry of \(\widehat G_y\); otherwise the cross-channel phase is destroyed.
  • Enough averages. One segment gives a noisy estimate with artificially low rank. Overlap adds segments but not independent averages.
  • Resolution. Segment length sets resolution; zero-padding only densifies the grid. A peak narrower than the resolution cannot be separated from its neighbor.
  • Check stability. Plot \(s_1(f)\) and \(s_1/s_2\); a peak that moves with segment length or averaging is not stable.

9.3Interpreting the decomposition

  • Stable direction. The leading vector should be stable by MAC across the peak; rapid rotation means a mixture.
  • Rank one. A large \(s_1/s_2\) supports the interpretation; a ratio near one leaves the direction undetermined.
  • Harmonic forcing. A sinusoid also gives a narrow rank-one peak. Check whether the frequency matches a known forcing frequency and whether the shape is structural.
  • Visibility. An absent peak does not prove an absent mode; it may be weakly excited or invisible at the sensors.
  • Nonlinearity. Harmonics, combination frequencies, and amplitude-dependent shapes violate the linear model.

9.4Reporting

Report sensor coordinates and directions, measured quantity and units, shape normalization and phase convention, cross-spectrum convention, segment length, window, overlap, number of averages, and resolution. For EFDD also report the bell, MAC threshold, and fitted interval. State whether reported frequencies are damped \(f_d\) or natural \(f_n\); they differ by \(\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). When a check fails, report the mixture or bias rather than presenting the leading vector as a mode shape.

AState-space linearization

The quadratic eigenvalue problem (3.2) can be written as an ordinary eigenproblem by including velocity in the state. Let \(z\) be the \(2n\)-component state vector and \(I\) the \(n\times n\) identity. With zero blocks of the same size, the free equation \(M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=0\) becomes

\[z=\begin{bmatrix}q\\\dot q\end{bmatrix},\qquad \dot z=Az,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\-M^{-1}K&-M^{-1}C\end{bmatrix}.\]
(A.1)

The upper block of \(\dot z=Az\) is \(\dot q=\dot q\), an identity. The lower block is \(\ddot q=-M^{-1}Kq-M^{-1}C\dot q\), which is the free equation multiplied by \(M^{-1}\). The matrix \(A\) is \(2n\times2n\), so it has \(2n\) eigenvalues counted with multiplicity. They are the same eigenvalues as the quadratic problem, as the next calculation shows.

Seek a state solution \(z=\Psi e^{\lambda t}\). Then \(\dot z=\lambda\Psi e^{\lambda t}\), and cancelling the exponential gives \(A\Psi=\lambda\Psi\). Write the upper and lower \(n\)-component blocks of \(\Psi\) as \(\Psi_1\) and \(\Psi_2\). The two block equations are

\[\lambda\Psi_1=\Psi_2,\qquad -M^{-1}K\Psi_1-M^{-1}C\Psi_2=\lambda\Psi_2.\]
(A.2)

The first equation gives \(\Psi_2=\lambda\Psi_1\). Substitute into the second and multiply by \(M\):

\[-K\Psi_1-\lambda C\Psi_1=\lambda^2M\Psi_1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda C+K\right)\Psi_1=0.\]

Thus \(\Psi_1\) is a null vector of \(Z(\lambda)\), so it is a mode shape \(\phi_m\), and the state eigenvector is

\[\Psi_m=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix},\qquad Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0.\]

Conversely, every nonzero solution of the quadratic problem produces a state eigenvector by stacking \(\phi_m\) and \(\lambda_m\phi_m\). The correspondence is exact:

\[Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix}=\lambda_m\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix}.\]

In the quadratic problem, \(\lambda_m\) selects the matrix \(Z(\lambda_m)\) and \(\phi_m\) lies in its nullspace; \(\phi_m\) does not satisfy \(Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=\lambda_m\phi_m\). To recover a displacement mode shape from a state eigenvector, take its first \(n\) entries.

The two problems have the same \(2n\) eigenvalues, counting repeated roots according to multiplicity. Real matrices give conjugate pairs of nonreal roots. A defective eigenvalue has fewer independent eigenvectors than its multiplicity and requires polynomial factors in time; the simple-pole expansion used in this page excludes that case. The state form also explains why the exponential ansatz is sufficient: the solution of \(\dot z=Az\) is a matrix exponential, and the matrix exponential is a combination of \(e^{\lambda t}\) terms (with polynomial factors in the defective case).

A.1Damped modal parameters and undamped structural frequencies

There are two different meanings of “remove the damping.” For the equivalent scalar oscillator, we set its damping ratio to zero while keeping its frequency parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\). For the whole structure, we set \(C=0\) in (2.7) and solve a new eigenproblem. Let \(\Omega_r\) and \(\psi_r\) denote an undamped angular frequency and its displacement shape. Substituting \(q=\psi e^{j\Omega t}\) into the undamped equation gives

\[M\ddot q+Kq=0,\qquad K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r.\]
(A.3)

This is a generalized symmetric eigenproblem. The matrix \(M^{-1}K\) is not symmetric in the ordinary inner product, but it is symmetric in the \(M\)-inner product, and its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative when \(M\succ0\) and \(K\succeq0\). For a single mass and spring, (A.3) and the scalar oscillator both give \(\sqrt{k/\mu}\).

They also agree mode by mode under proportional damping, \(C=aM+bK\) with constants \(a,b\). To see this, apply \(Z(\lambda)\) to an undamped mode \(\psi_r\) and use \(K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r\):

\[Z(\lambda)\psi_r=\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda aM+\lambda bK+K\right)\psi_r=\left[\lambda^2+a\lambda+\Omega_r^2(1+b\lambda)\right]M\psi_r.\]
(A.4)

Since \(M\psi_r\ne0\), the bracket must vanish. It is a quadratic in \(\lambda\),

\[\lambda^2+(a+b\Omega_r^2)\lambda+\Omega_r^2=0.\]

Comparing with \(\lambda^2+2\zeta_r\Omega_r\lambda+\Omega_r^2\) gives \(\omega_{n,r}=\Omega_r\) and \(\zeta_r=(a/\Omega_r+b\Omega_r)/2\). The damped mode is the undamped mode with an added decay, and the two notions of natural frequency coincide. For general damping this is not true: the damped modes can combine several undamped shapes, and \(|\lambda_m|\) need not equal any \(\Omega_r\). In this page, “natural frequency of mode \(m\)” always means the equivalent-oscillator parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\).

BThe residue formula via the adjugate

Section 4.4 shows that the residue is rank one, but it leaves the input-coupling vector \(\gamma_m\) in terms of a limit. This appendix evaluates that limit explicitly. The result is the formula used in implementations, and it shows exactly when a mode disappears from the transfer matrix.

The cofactor at row \(i\), column \(j\) of a square matrix is \((-1)^{i+j}\) times the determinant after deleting row \(i\) and column \(j\). The adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}Z\) is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. It remains defined at singular matrices and satisfies

\[Z\operatorname{adj}Z=\operatorname{adj}Z\,Z=(\det Z)I.\]
(B.1)

Wherever \(Z\) is invertible, \(Z^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}Z/\det Z\). The transfer matrix is therefore

\[H(s)=L\frac{\operatorname{adj}Z(s)}{\det Z(s)}B.\]

At a simple root \(\lambda_m\), the determinant has a simple zero and the adjugate has rank one. The rank statement follows from (B.1): at the root, \(\det Z=0\), so \(Z\operatorname{adj}Z=0\). Every column of the adjugate lies in the nullspace of \(Z(\lambda_m)\), which is one-dimensional, and the adjugate is not the zero matrix because the root is simple. Therefore

\[\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)=c_m\,\phi_m\ell_m^H,\]
(B.2)

where \(c_m\ne0\) is a constant, \(\phi_m\) is the right null vector, and \(\ell_m^H\) is the left null vector, satisfying \(Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0\) and \(\ell_m^HZ(\lambda_m)=0\). The two null vectors are nonzero because the root is simple.

Near the root, expand the determinant in a Taylor series. Let \(d'_m\) be its derivative at \(\lambda_m\). Then

\[\det Z(s)=(s-\lambda_m)d'_m+O\!\left((s-\lambda_m)^2\right).\]
(B.3)

The symbol \(O((s-\lambda_m)^2)\) denotes a remainder bounded by a constant times \(|s-\lambda_m|^2\) near the root. The derivative is given by Jacobi’s identity, which is valid also at singular matrices:

\[d'_m=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)Z'(\lambda_m)\right),\qquad Z'(s)=2sM+C.\]

Substituting (B.2) into the trace gives \(d'_m=c_m\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m\). Now form the residue limit. The factors \(s-\lambda_m\) cancel, as do the constants \(c_m\):

\[R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s)=\frac{L\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)B}{d'_m}=\frac{(L\phi_m)(\ell_m^HB)}{\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m}=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(B.4)

Thus the input-coupling vector is

\[\gamma_m^T=\frac{\ell_m^HB}{\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m}.\]

The denominator is nonzero at a simple root. If it were zero, the derivative of the determinant would vanish, and the root would not be simple. The residue vanishes precisely when the mode is unobserved, \(L\phi_m=0\), or unexcited, \(\ell_m^HB=0\). In a minimal state realization every state direction is reachable from the inputs and observable in the outputs, so no state pole is lost from the transfer matrix; individual entries can still omit modes.

For force-to-velocity and force-to-acceleration matrices, denote the transfer matrices by \(H_{\dot y}\) and \(H_{\ddot y}\). With zero initial conditions, differentiation becomes multiplication by \(s\), so \(H_{\dot y}=sH\). For acceleration, differentiating twice gives \(H_{\ddot y}=s^2H\). Its high-frequency limit is the feedthrough \(LM^{-1}B\); equivalently, \(H_{\ddot y}=LM^{-1}B+\tilde H(s)\) with \(\tilde H(s)\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\). The residues acquire factors \(\lambda_m\) and \(\lambda_m^2\), and the feedthrough adds a constant term that has no pole. Real or repeated roots require a different expansion; truncating the modal sum to a few modes leaves a residual.

Inverse expansion: [1], §3.5, equation (3.11). Factorized residues: [3], equations (2)–(3). The adjugate calculation supplies the intermediate steps.

CA bound on the omitted response

This appendix repeats the bound of §6.3 with the same notation, so that the main text and the proof can be read side by side. Assume the force spectral matrix \(G_x\) is constant across the band and denote it by \(Q_x\). Let \(Q_x^{1/2}\) be its Hermitian positive-semidefinite square root. Define the excitation-weighted selected term and remainder

\[P=\frac{v_m\gamma_m^T}{j\omega-\lambda_m},\qquad E=E_m(j\omega),\qquad P_w=PQ_x^{1/2},\qquad E_w=EQ_x^{1/2}.\]
(C.1)

Expanding (5.3) gives

\[G_y=P_wP_w^H+P_wE_w^H+E_wP_w^H+E_wE_w^H.\]

Let \(\|\cdot\|_2\) be the matrix spectral norm. The triangle inequality and the submultiplicative property \(\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2\) give

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le2\|P_w\|_2\|E_w\|_2+\|E_w\|_2^2.\]

If \(\|E_w\|_2\le\varepsilon\|P_w\|_2\), then

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)\|P_w\|_2^2,\qquad \|P_w\|_2^2=\left\|P_wP_w^H\right\|_2.\]
(C.2)

The second equality says that the bound is relative to the size of the selected term itself, not to an unrelated scale. The number \(\varepsilon\) is a sufficient condition for an accurate rank-one approximation. It is not necessary: a matrix can be close to rank one even when this particular bound is loose. What the bound makes clear is the dependence on the excitation. A small \(\varepsilon\) requires the omitted response, weighted by the force spectrum, to be small compared with the selected response, also weighted by the force spectrum. Light damping alone does not produce that condition.

DSpectral conventions and estimation

The main text uses one convention for the cross-spectrum and one normalization for the spectral density. This appendix states both precisely, derives the normalization, and records the pitfalls that change a shape or a damping estimate.

D.1Conjugation order

Some sources define the cross-spectral matrix with \(\bar YY^T\) instead of \(YY^H\). Denote that matrix by \(\widetilde G_y\). Since \(Y^H=\bar Y^T\),

\[\widetilde G_y=\bar YY^T=G_y^T=\bar G_y.\]
(D.1)

Under this convention, the rank-one term becomes \(\widetilde G_y\approx d_m\bar v_mv_m^T\). The leading vector estimates \(\bar v_m\), so it must be conjugated to recover the shape convention used here. Both conventions are valid; mixing them within one calculation is not. SciPy’s csd(a, b) uses conjugate-first ordering. If y[i] contains channel \(i\), the entry \((i,j)\) of this page’s matrix is obtained with csd(y[j], y[i]). [6]

D.2Welch’s estimate

Welch’s method divides a record into possibly overlapping segments, applies the same window to every channel, and averages the spectral products. Detrending removes a fitted mean or trend. A window assigns a real weight to each sample to control the spectral spreading caused by a finite record.

Let \(N_b\) be the number of segments, \(N_w\) their length in samples, and \(w[\nu]\) the window at within-segment index \(\nu\). Indices \(b\) and \(k\) label segments and frequency bins. Write \(Y_b[k]\) for the vector of unnormalized discrete Fourier transform (DFT) coefficients after detrending and windowing. A fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the DFT efficiently. The one-sided factor \(\eta_k\) is \(2\) at interior positive-frequency bins and \(1\) at DC and Nyquist, when present. At sampling rate \(f_s\), the estimate is

\[\widehat G_y^+[k]=\frac{\eta_k}{N_b f_s\sum_{\nu=0}^{N_w-1}w[\nu]^2}\sum_{b=1}^{N_b}Y_b[k]Y_b[k]^H.\]
(D.2)

The normalization is easiest to check on white noise. Suppose a channel is white with variance \(\sigma^2\) and sampling rate \(f_s\). Its two-sided PSD is constant, \(G=\sigma^2/f_s\), because integrating a constant over the Nyquist band \([-f_s/2,f_s/2]\) gives \(Gf_s=\sigma^2\). For one segment, the expected squared magnitude of the windowed DFT coefficient is \(\mathbb E|Y_b[k]|^2=\sigma^2\sum_\nu w[\nu]^2\). Substituting \(G=\sigma^2/f_s\) gives

\[\mathbb E|Y_b[k]|^2=G\,f_s\sum_{\nu=0}^{N_w-1}w[\nu]^2.\]

Dividing by \(f_s\sum w^2\) therefore gives an unbiased estimate of \(G\), and averaging over \(N_b\) segments reduces its variance. The factor \(\eta_k\) converts the two-sided density to the one-sided convention. For a colored signal the window convolves the spectrum, so the estimate is a smoothed version of the true density; the normalization preserves the integral, not the exact shape.

Using identical segments and windows preserves the Hermitian positive-semidefinite structure. Each segment contributes at most one independent direction to the average, so the estimated rank cannot exceed \(N_b\). This is a finite-data rank, not a physical one. Averaging reduces variability between estimates, but it does not eliminate a persistent noise or forcing spectrum. Longer segments improve frequency resolution; more effectively independent segments improve averaging. Overlap does not make segments independent. Appending zeros before the DFT only samples the same windowed spectrum more densely; it does not improve the resolution set by the segment duration and window. [5]

D.3Density units and the inverse transform

The spectra here are densities per hertz. With superscripts \((f)\) and \((\omega)\) labeling density per hertz and per radian per second, the change of variables \(d\omega=2\pi\,df\) gives

\[G^{(\omega)}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}G^{(f)}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right).\]
(D.3)

For the EFDD inverse transform, the one-sided spectrum must be mirrored to negative frequencies before the inverse DFT. Halve the one-sided interior positive bins, copy them to the corresponding negative bins, and keep the DC and Nyquist endpoint weights from (D.2). With the inverse DFT normalization \(1/N\), the correlation is \(f_s\) times the inverse DFT of the mirrored spectrum, because the frequency spacing is \(\Delta f=f_s/N\) and the inverse transform sums \(\Delta f\,G[k]e^{j2\pi k\ell/N}\). Normalizing by \(\mathcal R_m(0)\) removes an overall scale but does not repair incorrect mirroring or incorrect endpoint weights. An incorrect one-sided scaling is a common cause of a biased damping estimate. [5]

D.4Additive sensor noise

Let \(\eta(t)\) denote sensor noise and \(G_\eta\) its PSD. If the noise and structural response have zero cross-covariance at every lag, their spectra add:

\[G_{y+\eta}=G_y+G_\eta.\]
(D.4)

Correlated noise introduces additional cross terms. A large singular value can therefore reflect the measurement process as well as the structure. Noise raises all singular values, but it affects the smaller ones proportionally more, so it reduces the ratio \(s_1/s_2\) that the rank-one interpretation relies on.

EA perturbation bound for the leading singular vector

Section 7.2 claims that the leading singular vector is close to the physical shape when the perturbation is small. This appendix makes that statement precise. The argument is short and gives the main dependence; a sharper version with the spectral gap is the Davis–Kahan theorem. [11]

Let \(G=G_0+E\) be Hermitian, with \(G_0=d\,vv^H\), \(\|v\|=1\), and \(d>0\). Let \(u\) be a unit eigenvector of \(G\) for its largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\), and assume \(\lambda_1>0\). Decompose \(u\) into a component along \(v\) and a component orthogonal to it:

\[u=\alpha v+\beta w,\qquad w\perp v,\qquad \|w\|=1,\qquad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.\]

The angle \(\theta\) between \(u\) and \(v\) satisfies \(\sin\theta=|\beta|\). Because \(u\) is an eigenvector, \(Gu=\lambda_1u\). Take the inner product with \(w\):

\[\lambda_1\beta=w^HGu=w^H(G_0+E)u.\]

The unperturbed term vanishes: \(w^HG_0u=d\,w^Hv(v^Hu)=0\), because \(w\perp v\). Therefore \(\lambda_1\beta=w^HEu\), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\[|\beta|\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1},\qquad \sin\theta\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1}.\]
(E.1)

This is the basic bound. It says that the angular error is controlled by the perturbation relative to the leading eigenvalue. Since \(\lambda_1\) is approximately \(d\), and \(s_1\) estimates \(\lambda_1\), a large \(s_1\) relative to the perturbation is favourable.

A sharper form replaces \(\lambda_1\) by the gap between the first and second eigenvalues. If the leading eigenvalue is simple and the perturbation does not close the gap, the Davis–Kahan theorem gives

\[\sin\theta\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}.\]

When the second eigenvalue is close to the first, the denominator is small and the bound is weak, which is the quantitative version of the statement that a ratio \(s_1/s_2\) near one leaves the direction undetermined. When the gap is large, the direction is stable even if the absolute perturbation is not tiny. The bound is sufficient, not necessary: a direction can be stable for reasons this inequality does not capture.
