FDD at a glance

FDD uses vibration records from two or more synchronized sensor channels. It estimates a spectral matrix for the measured responses. It then decomposes this matrix at each frequency.

What you can estimate

  • Frequency: a peak in the first singular value can indicate a resonance.
  • Mode shape: the leading singular vector near an isolated peak estimates the relative motion at the sensors.
  • Damping: enhanced FDD (EFDD) estimates damping from the decay of a selected spectral bell.

The measured shape has an arbitrary scale and phase. An FDD peak is an estimate, and it is not always equal to the natural frequency. See Section 7.3.

How to use this guide

For the procedure, go to FDD, step by step. For measurement and interpretation checks, go to Section 9. For the derivation, start with the assumptions and continue through Sections 1 to 8.

Check each result. A peak can come from a harmonic force or from more than one mode. Noise, sensor placement, and spectral settings also affect the estimate. Use the checks in Section 9 before you report a mode.

Assumptions

The derivation uses the assumptions below. A7 concerns the spectral estimate. A9 and A10 define the approximations for FDD and EFDD. The relevant sections identify where each assumption is used. See also the checks in Section 9.

Model

A0
Output-only. The sensors measure only \(y(t)\). The input \(x(t)\) is unknown and has a random model.
A1
Linear, time-invariant, finite-dimensional. The model has \(n\) coordinates, \(r\) inputs, and \(p\) sensors. Displacements from equilibrium are small. The matrices \(M,C,K\) are constant.
A2
Viscous damping. The matrix \(C\) is constant and symmetric. General damping makes the modes complex. The residue derivation still applies.
A3
Linear input and output maps. Forces enter as \(Bx\). Sensors measure \(Lq\). The model excludes sensor dynamics, delays, and nonlinearities.
A4
Matrix properties. \(M=M^T\succ0\), \(K=K^T\succeq0\), \(C=C^T\succeq0\); \(K\succ0\) if restrained. Derived from energy in §2.4.
A5
Simple, stable eigenvalues. The \(2n\) eigenvalues are distinct and stable. They occur in conjugate pairs. Each pole is simple. Repeated or defective roots are excluded.

Excitation and measurement

A6
Stationary random excitation. The input \(x\) has zero mean and is wide-sense stationary. It has finite variance and a spectral density matrix. Ergodicity lets time averages estimate ensemble averages.
A7
Accurate estimate. \(\widehat G_y\) is close to \(G_y\); finite-record errors are treated in Appendix D.
A8
Clean, synchronized measurements. Noise is zero or uncorrelated with the response. The channels are synchronized and calibrated.
A9
One dominant mode in the band. \(G_x(f)\approx Q_x\), and the residual \(E_m\) is small relative to the selected term. Made precise in §6.
A10
One mode in the bell. The EFDD bell contains one mode and its correlation decays as a single exponential.

Conventions

A11
Fourier and spectral conventions. \(j^2=-1\); Fourier transform \(e^{-j2\pi ft}\); \(G_y\) two-sided unless marked \(^+\); one-sided spectra double interior bins but not DC or Nyquist; \(f\) in hertz, \(\omega=2\pi f\); displacement PSD in \(\text{unit}^2/\text{Hz}\).
A12
Scale and phase are not identified. The shape is known only up to a complex scale. The modal mass cannot be identified.

Part IThe model

1The measurement problem

Sensors record the response \(y(t)\). The forces \(x(t)\) are unknown. The aim is to estimate the vibration frequencies, the mode shapes, and the decay rates. A mode shape describes the relative amplitude and phase at the sensors. A single mode has a fixed spatial pattern. When several modes contribute, the pattern can vary with frequency.

FDD estimates the cross-spectral matrix \(G_y(f)\) and decomposes it at each frequency. Near a peak, the dominant direction can estimate a mode shape. EFDD selects a band around a peak and transforms it into a correlation function. The decay of that function gives a damping estimate. The shape is known only up to a complex scale. See §6.

2Energy and the equation of motion

This section derives the equation of motion from energy. It also uses Lagrange’s equations to check the result. The energy model gives the matrix properties used later.

2.1Energy of a vibrating structure

Let \(q(t)\in\mathbb R^n\) contain the displacements from static equilibrium. For the linear model, the energy terms are:

\[T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q,\qquad V=\tfrac12q^TKq,\qquad D=\tfrac12\dot q^TC\dot q.\]
(2.1)

The matrices \(M\), \(K\), and \(C\) represent mass, stiffness, and viscous damping. The external forces do virtual work:

\[\delta W=(Bx)^T\delta q=x^TB^T\delta q,\]

The input matrix \(B\) has size \(n\times r\). The product \(Bx\) is the generalized force. The sensor model is:

\[y=Lq,\]

The output matrix \(L\) has size \(p\times n\). A Taylor expansion about equilibrium gives the quadratic forms. At a stationary point, the linear term is zero. We can set the constant term to zero. For small displacements, the quadratic term dominates (A1). The energy matrices contain second derivatives and are symmetric.

2.2One degree of freedom

Consider a mass \(\mu>0\), spring \(k>0\), and damper \(b\ge0\). Let \(F(t)\) be the force and \(u(t)\) the displacement. The energy terms and virtual work are:

\[T=\tfrac12\mu\dot u^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12ku^2,\qquad D=\tfrac12b\dot u^2,\qquad \delta W=F\,\delta u.\]
(2.2)

The rate of stored energy equals the supplied power minus the dissipated power:

\[\frac{d}{dt}(T+V)=F\dot u-b\dot u^2.\]
(2.3)

Differentiate the energy terms and factor the result:

\[\mu\dot u\ddot u+ku\dot u=F\dot u-b\dot u^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\dot u\bigl(\mu\ddot u+b\dot u+ku-F\bigr)=0.\]

When \(\dot u\ne0\), divide by \(\dot u\). At an isolated zero of \(\dot u\), continuity of \(\ddot u\) and \(F\) gives the same result by a limit:

\[\mu\ddot u+b\dot u+ku=F.\]
(2.4)

Each term is a force. Divide by \(\mu\) to get the normalized equation:

\[\ddot u+\frac{b}{\mu}\dot u+\frac{k}{\mu}u=\frac{F}{\mu}.\]

Lagrange’s equations also give this result. They extend to a matrix model. Use the Lagrangian \(T-V\), the dissipation function \(D\), and the generalized force \(Q\):

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}-\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot u}=Q.\]
(2.5)

For this oscillator, \(T-V=\tfrac12\mu\dot u^2-\tfrac12ku^2\), \(D=\tfrac12b\dot u^2\), and \(Q=F\). The derivatives are:

\[\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}=\mu\dot u,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}=\mu\ddot u,\qquad \frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial u}=-ku,\qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot u}=b\dot u.\]

Substitute these derivatives to get (2.4).

2.3Many degrees of freedom

For the full structure, the energy balance is:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q+\tfrac12q^TKq\right)=\dot q^TBx-\dot q^TC\dot q.\]
(2.6)

The matrices \(M\) and \(K\) are constant and symmetric. Thus:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\right)=\dot q^TM\ddot q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12q^TKq\right)=\dot q^TKq.\]

Collect the terms:

\[\dot q^T\bigl(M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq-Bx\bigr)=0.\]

This equation expresses zero net power along a motion. The energy balance alone does not determine all components of the force equation from one velocity. Lagrange’s equations give the vector equation directly:

\[M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=Bx,\qquad y=Lq.\]
(2.7)

2.4What the matrices mean

Symmetry. The equality \(q^TAq=q^TA^Tq\) shows that only the symmetric part contributes to a quadratic form. The energy model gives symmetric \(M\) and \(K\). Assumption A2 specifies a symmetric \(C\).

Definiteness. A matrix \(A\) is positive semidefinite if \(x^TAx\ge0\). It is positive definite if \(x^TAx>0\) for all \(x\ne0\). Here \(T\ge0\), and \(T=0\) only when \(\dot q=0\). Thus \(M\succ0\) and \(M\) is invertible. The strain energy \(V\ge0\). A restrained structure has \(K\succ0\). A free-free structure has \(K\succeq0\) and rigid-body modes with \(K\phi=0\). Also \(D\ge0\), so \(C\succeq0\). The damping matrix can be singular.

Units. Let \(q\) be in meters and \(x\) in newtons. Then \(M\) is in kilograms, \(C\) is in newton-seconds per meter, and \(K\) is in newtons per meter. Each term in (2.7) is a force. The matrices \(B\) and \(L\) account for input and sensor units.

Two masses. Consider masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\). Spring \(k_1\) and damper \(c_1\) connect the wall to \(m_1\). Spring \(k_2\) and damper \(c_2\) connect the masses. Spring \(k_3\) and damper \(c_3\) connect \(m_2\) to the wall. Let \(q=[q_1,q_2]^T\). Then:

\[M=\begin{bmatrix}m_1&0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}k_1+k_2&-k_2\\-k_2&k_2+k_3\end{bmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{bmatrix}c_1+c_2&-c_2\\-c_2&c_2+c_3\end{bmatrix}.\]
(2.8)

The off-diagonal entries are negative. The spring and damper between the masses oppose their relative motion. The energy terms are:

\[V=\tfrac12\left[k_1q_1^2+k_2(q_2-q_1)^2+k_3q_2^2\right],\qquad D=\tfrac12\left[c_1\dot q_1^2+c_2(\dot q_2-\dot q_1)^2+c_3\dot q_2^2\right].\]

Each bracket is a sum of squares with positive coefficients. Thus \(V,D\ge0\). The matrix \(K\succ0\) when \(k_1+k_2>0\) and \(k_1k_2+k_1k_3+k_2k_3>0\). These conditions hold for positive springs. A force on \(m_1\) gives \(B=[1,0]^T\). If both displacements are measured, \(L=I\).

2.5Lagrange’s equations for the matrix case

Use \(q\) as the generalized coordinate and \(Q=Bx\) as the generalized force:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot q}=Q.\]
(2.9)

The kinetic energy is \(T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\). The strain energy \(V\) does not depend on \(\dot q\). Thus:

\[\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}=M\dot q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}=M\ddot q,\]

This result uses a constant \(M\). The strain energy is \(V=\tfrac12q^TKq\). Component \(i\) of its gradient is:

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i}=\frac12\sum_{j,k}K_{jk}\frac{\partial(q_jq_k)}{\partial q_i}=\frac12\sum_kK_{ik}q_k+\frac12\sum_jK_{ji}q_j=(Kq)_i,\]

This result uses \(K_{ji}=K_{ij}\) and a change of index. Also, \(\partial D/\partial\dot q=C\dot q\). Substitute these terms and \(\partial(T-V)/\partial q=-\partial V/\partial q\) into (2.9):

\[M\ddot q+Kq+C\dot q=Bx,\]

This is (2.7). Lagrange’s equation permits a non-diagonal \(M\).

3Modal analysis: free vibration

Free vibration is motion without an applied force. A mode is a spatial pattern that evolves at one complex rate.

3.1The exponential ansatz and the quadratic eigenproblem

Set \(x=0\) in (2.7):

\[M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=0.\]

This linear equation has constant coefficients and exponential solutions. Repeated eigenvalues can also give polynomial factors. Assumption A5 excludes repeated eigenvalues. Use:

\[q_c(t)=\phi e^{\lambda t},\qquad \dot q_c=\lambda\phi e^{\lambda t},\qquad \ddot q_c=\lambda^2\phi e^{\lambda t}.\]
(3.1)

The subscript \(c\) identifies a complex auxiliary solution. The nonzero vector \(\phi\) is its spatial pattern. The scalar \(\lambda\) is its complex rate. Substitute these terms into the free-vibration equation:

\[\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda C+K\right)\phi\,e^{\lambda t}=0.\]

Cancel the exponential term and define:

\[Z(s)=s^2M+sC+K,\qquad Z(\lambda)\phi=0,\qquad \det Z(\lambda)=0.\]
(3.2)

The nonzero vector \(\phi\) makes \(Z(\lambda)\) singular. A zero determinant gives a null vector. The scale of \(\phi\) is arbitrary; its direction gives the shape. This is a quadratic eigenvalue problem. The leading term of \(\det Z(s)\) is \(\det(M)s^{2n}\). Thus the polynomial has degree \(2n\) and has \(2n\) roots. The real matrices give conjugate pairs. A numerical solver can use the \(2n\times2n\) state form \(z=[q;\dot q]\). Its eigenvectors stack \(\phi_m\) on \(\lambda_m\phi_m\). See Appendix A.

3.2Real motion from complex modes

The auxiliary solution \(q_c\) is complex, but the physical displacement \(q\) is real. The real matrices also give the conjugate pair \(\bar\lambda\) and \(\bar\phi\). Combine the pair:

\[q_m(t)=c\,\phi e^{\lambda t}+\bar c\,\bar\phi e^{\bar\lambda t}=2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(c\,\phi e^{\lambda t}\right).\]
(3.3)

This result uses \(z+\bar z=2\operatorname{Re}z\). Each coordinate is real. The \(n\) coefficients \(c_m\) contain \(2n\) real constants. These constants match the initial conditions. Write \(\lambda=\sigma+j\omega_d\):

\[\lambda=\sigma+j\omega_d,\qquad e^{\lambda t}=e^{\sigma t}\left[\cos(\omega_d t)+j\sin(\omega_d t)\right].\]
(3.4)

Stability requires \(\sigma<0\). Choose \(\omega_d>0\) and write \(\lambda=-\alpha+j\omega_d\), with \(\alpha>0\). The parameter \(\alpha\) is the decay rate. The parameter \(\omega_d\) is the damped angular frequency. For \(C=0\) or proportional damping, \(\phi\) can be real. For general \(C\), it is complex and describes relative phases. The physical displacement remains real.

3.3The equivalent single-degree-of-freedom oscillator

The natural frequency and damping ratio come from a scalar oscillator. Let \(\mu>0\), \(k>0\), and \(b\ge0\). Set \(F=0\) in (2.4) and divide by \(\mu\):

\[\ddot u+\frac{b}{\mu}\dot u+\frac{k}{\mu}u=0.\]
(3.5)

With \(b=0\), the equation is \(\ddot u+(k/\mu)u=0\). Thus \(\omega_n^2=k/\mu\) and \(f_n=\omega_n/(2\pi)\). Restore the damper and substitute \(u=e^{st}\):

\[\mu s^2+bs+k=0,\qquad s=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4\mu k}}{2\mu}.\]
(3.6)

The discriminant determines the roots. If \(b^2<4\mu k\), the roots are complex and the response oscillates. If \(b^2>4\mu k\), the roots are negative and real; the response does not oscillate. Equality gives a double root. At this boundary, \(b_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{\mu k}\):

\[b_{\mathrm{crit}}^2-4\mu k=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{\mu k},\qquad \zeta\equiv\frac{b}{b_{\mathrm{crit}}}=\frac{b}{2\sqrt{\mu k}}.\]

The damping ratio \(\zeta\) has no units. The response is underdamped when \(\zeta<1\), critically damped when \(\zeta=1\), and overdamped when \(\zeta>1\). The factor 2 comes from (3.6). Since \(b/\mu=2\zeta\omega_n\), the normalized free-vibration equation and its characteristic polynomial are:

\[\ddot u+2\zeta\omega_n\dot u+\omega_n^2u=0,\qquad s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=0.\]

For \(\zeta<1\), the roots are \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). Compare them with \(\lambda=-\alpha\pm j\omega_d\). Then \(\alpha=\zeta\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). The full polynomial is:

\[(s-\lambda)(s-\bar\lambda)=(s+\alpha)^2+\omega_d^2=s^2+2\alpha s+(\alpha^2+\omega_d^2),\]

Thus \(\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}=\alpha_m\) and \(\omega_{n,m}^2=\alpha_m^2+\omega_{d,m}^2\). The modal parameters are:

\[\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|=\sqrt{\alpha_m^2+\omega_{d,m}^2},\qquad \zeta_m=\frac{\alpha_m}{\omega_{n,m}},\qquad \omega_{d,m}=\omega_{n,m}\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}.\]
(3.7)

The value \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\) is a parameter of the equivalent oscillator. It does not necessarily equal an undamped structural frequency. See §3.5.

3.4Interactive: damping regimes

Release the oscillator from \(u(0)=1\) and \(\dot u(0)=0\). For \(\zeta<1\), the response is \(u=e^{-\alpha t}(C_1\cos\omega_dt+C_2\sin\omega_dt)\). The initial conditions give \(C_1=1\) and \(C_2=\alpha/\omega_d\). For \(\zeta=1\), the response is \(u=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\omega_nt}\). The conditions give \(C_1=1\) and \(C_2=\omega_n\). For \(\zeta>1\), define \(\beta=\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\) and \(\lambda_{1,2}=-\alpha\pm\beta\). The three responses are:

\[u(t)=\begin{cases}e^{-\alpha t}\left[\cos(\omega_dt)+\dfrac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt)\right],&0\le\zeta<1,\\(1+\omega_nt)e^{-\omega_nt},&\zeta=1,\\\dfrac{-\lambda_2e^{\lambda_1t}+\lambda_1e^{\lambda_2t}}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2},&\zeta>1.\end{cases}\]
(3.8)

The underdamped response approaches the critical response as \(\zeta\to1\). The overdamped response also approaches it as \(\beta\to0\). The figure shows the poles and the response.

3.5Two meanings of natural frequency

There are two ways to remove damping. For the equivalent oscillator, set \(\zeta=0\) and keep \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\). For the structure, set \(C=0\) in (2.7) and solve:

\[M\ddot q+Kq=0,\qquad K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r.\]

For one mass and spring, both methods give \(\sqrt{k/\mu}\). Under proportional damping, \(C=aM+bK\), the damped modes have the undamped shapes and an added decay. Appendix A shows that \(\omega_{n,r}=\Omega_r\) and \(\zeta_r=(a/\Omega_r+b\Omega_r)/2\). General damping can mix the undamped shapes. Then \(|\lambda_m|\) need not equal any \(\Omega_r\). This page uses \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\).

Part IIFrom forces to spectra

4The transfer matrix and its residues

Free vibration gives the poles. The transfer matrix maps forces to sensor measurements in the frequency domain. Its residues describe the contribution of each pole.

4.1The Laplace transform

For a signal \(q(t)\) on \(t\ge0\), define the Laplace transform:

\[Q(s)=\mathcal L\{q\}(s)=\int_0^\infty q(t)e^{-st}\,dt,\qquad s=\sigma+j\omega.\]
(4.1)

This is the Fourier transform of \(q(t)e^{-\sigma t}\). Choose \(\sigma\) large enough for the integral to converge. Integration by parts gives the derivative rule. Use \(u=e^{-st}\) and \(dv=\dot q\,dt\):

\[\int_0^\infty\dot q(t)e^{-st}\,dt=\bigl[q(t)e^{-st}\bigr]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty q(t)e^{-st}\,dt=sQ(s)-q(0).\]

The upper limit is zero for the selected \(\sigma\). The lower limit gives \(-q(0)\). Apply the rule again:

\[\mathcal L\{\dot q\}(s)=sQ(s)-q(0),\qquad \mathcal L\{\ddot q\}(s)=s^2Q(s)-sq(0)-\dot q(0).\]
(4.2)

For zero initial conditions, each time derivative becomes multiplication by \(s\). The initial-condition response can be added separately. On the imaginary axis, \(s=j\omega=j2\pi f\), the transfer function gives the frequency response.

4.2From the equation of motion to \(H(s)\)

Apply the Laplace transform to (2.7) with zero initial conditions:

\[\underbrace{(s^2M+sC+K)}_{Z(s)}Q(s)=BX(s),\qquad Y(s)=LQ(s).\]
(4.3)

When \(Z(s)\) is invertible:

\[Q(s)=Z(s)^{-1}BX(s),\qquad Y(s)=LZ(s)^{-1}BX(s),\]

so

\[Y(s)=H(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=LZ(s)^{-1}B.\]
(4.4)

The transfer matrix \(H\) has size \(p\times r\). Entry \(H_{ik}\) maps input \(k\) to sensor \(i\). Its entries have a common denominator, \(\det Z(s)\). The matrix \(Z\) is singular at the eigenvalues. A pole can cancel if the input or output map does not couple to that mode.

4.3Partial fractions at a simple pole

For one entry with a simple pole at \(\lambda_m\), write:

\[H_{ik}(s)=\frac{r_{ik,m}}{s-\lambda_m}+g_{ik,m}(s),\]
(4.5)

The remainder \(g_{ik,m}\) is finite at \(\lambda_m\). This separation is exact. Multiply by \(s-\lambda_m\) and take the limit:

\[\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H_{ik}(s)=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}\left[r_{ik,m}+(s-\lambda_m)g_{ik,m}(s)\right]=r_{ik,m}.\]
(4.6)

The second term goes to zero. Thus the limit gives the residue. Apply the result to each matrix entry:

\[H(s)=\frac{R_m}{s-\lambda_m}+H_{\mathrm{rest},m}(s),\qquad R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s).\]

The residue \(R_m\) has size \(p\times r\). The remainder \(H_{\mathrm{rest},m}\) contains the conjugate pole and all other modes. For a simple pole (A5), the principal part has one term.

4.4Why the residue is rank one

Separate the transfer from force to coordinates from the sensor map:

\[\mathcal T(s)=Z(s)^{-1}B,\qquad Q(s)=\mathcal T(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=L\mathcal T(s).\]

Let \(W_m\) be the residue of \(\mathcal T\) at \(\lambda_m\):

\[W_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)\mathcal T(s).\]

Multiply \(Z(s)\mathcal T(s)=B\) by \(s-\lambda_m\) and take the limit:

\[Z(\lambda_m)W_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)B=0.\]
(4.7)

Each column of \(W_m\) is in the nullspace of \(Z(\lambda_m)\). The nullspace contains the vectors that \(Z(\lambda_m)\) maps to zero. At a simple eigenvalue, that nullspace has one dimension. Thus each column is a multiple of \(\phi_m\). Let \(\gamma_{m,k}\) be the scalar for input \(k\):

\[W_m=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\gamma_{m,1}&\cdots&\phi_m\gamma_{m,r}\end{bmatrix}=\phi_m\gamma_m^T,\qquad \gamma_m^T=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{m,1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m,r}\end{bmatrix}.\]

The matrix \(L\) is constant. Apply it to the limit:

\[R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s)=L\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)\mathcal T(s)=LW_m=(L\phi_m)\gamma_m^T=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(4.8)

The residue has rank at most one: each column is a multiple of the measured shape \(v_m\). If \(v_m=0\), the sensors do not see the mode. If \(\gamma_m=0\), the inputs do not excite it. In either case the residue is zero.

For example, let two input coefficients be \(2\) and \(-3\), and let \(v_m=[1,-2]^T\):

\[R_m=\begin{bmatrix}2v_m&-3v_m\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-3\\-4&6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&-3\end{bmatrix}.\]

Both columns keep the ratio \(1:-2\). An input vector combines them into \(v_m(2X_1-3X_2)\). The full transfer matrix contains the other modes and need not have rank one. The rank statement applies to one residue.

4.5The full modal sum

For real matrices, the conjugate pole has residue \(\bar R_m\). Sum the \(n\) pairs:

\[H(s)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\frac{R_m}{s-\lambda_m}+\frac{\bar R_m}{s-\bar\lambda_m}\right),\qquad R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(4.9)

This sum is exact under A5. It does not assume that one mode dominates. A zero \(v_m\), zero \(\gamma_m\), or cancellation removes a term from \(H\). The mode still exists.

5The response spectral matrix

Operational forces are unknown and can vary between records. The spectral matrix is an average of products of Fourier coefficients.

5.1From records to a spectral matrix

Let \(T\) be the record duration and \(y(t)\) the measurement from \(p\) channels. Define:

\[Y_T(f)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}y(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt,\qquad G_y(f)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb E\!\left[Y_T(f)Y_T(f)^H\right].\]
(5.1)

The transform \(Y_T\) is random. The operator \(\mathbb E\) denotes an average over records. Entry \((i,k)\) of \(Y_TY_T^H\) is \(Y_i\bar Y_k\). The factor \(1/T\) gives a density in amplitude squared per hertz. The integral of a diagonal entry over all frequencies gives the channel mean square. It equals the variance when the mean is zero.

This is a two-sided spectral matrix. For real signals, negative frequencies mirror positive frequencies. A one-sided spectrum is marked \(^+\). It doubles interior positive bins but does not double DC or Nyquist. Assumption A6 specifies wide-sense stationarity and a spectral density. The expected products then depend only on time separation. Ergodicity permits a time average to estimate the ensemble average. See Appendix D for estimation details.

5.2Hermitian positive semidefiniteness

For any \(Y\),

\[\left(YY^H\right)^H=\left(Y^H\right)^HY^H=YY^H.\]

A matrix is Hermitian if it equals its conjugate transpose. Expectation preserves this property, so \(G_y\) is Hermitian. Its diagonal entries are averages of \(|Y_i|^2\). They are real and nonnegative. For any complex weight vector \(a\), \(|a^HY_T|^2\ge0\). The average gives:

\[G_y^H=G_y,\qquad a^HG_ya=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb E\!\left[|a^HY_T|^2\right]\ge0.\]
(5.2)

Thus \(G_y\) is Hermitian positive semidefinite. The spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis and real eigenvalues. Positive semidefiniteness makes those eigenvalues nonnegative. With unitary \(U\) and diagonal \(\Lambda\):

\[G_y=U\Lambda U^H,\qquad U^HU=I,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p),\quad \lambda_k\ge0.\]

For this matrix, the singular values are the eigenvalues. The left singular vectors are eigenvectors. The rank is the number of nonzero eigenvalues. A rank-one \(p\times p\) matrix has one nonzero eigenvalue and a nullspace of dimension \(p-1\).

5.3The input–output relation

For a sufficiently long record of the stationary forced response, the Fourier-domain relation is approximately:

\[Y_T(f)\approx H(j2\pi f)X_T(f).\]

This approximation follows from (4.4) after transients decay. For finite records, boundary effects generally prevent exact equality. Substitute the approximate relation into the outer product:

\[Y_TY_T^H\approx\left(HX_T\right)\left(HX_T\right)^H=HX_TX_T^HH^H.\]

At a fixed frequency, \(H\) is constant across realizations. It can therefore pass through the expectation. Apply (5.1) in the infinite-record limit:

\[G_y(f)=H(j2\pi f)\,G_x(f)\,H(j2\pi f)^H.\]
(5.3)

The input spectral matrix \(G_x\) has size \(r\times r\). It is Hermitian positive semidefinite. The output spectral matrix \(G_y\) has size \(p\times p\). The matrix \(G_x\) describes the excitation. The transfer matrix \(H\) describes the structural response and the sensors.

5.4Conventions and estimation

This page defines entry \((i,k)\) as \(Y_i\bar Y_k\). Some software uses \(\bar Y_iY_k\). That convention gives the transpose and conjugate of this matrix, and the estimated shape is conjugated. Do not mix the two conventions. A one-sided spectrum keeps \(f\ge0\) and doubles the interior bins. Its integral matches the two-sided mean square. Do not double DC or Nyquist. See Appendix D for the Welch estimator, its normalization, and the resolution–variance tradeoff.

Part IIIFinding modes in data

6When one mode dominates

The response matrix contains rank-one terms and their cross terms. FDD uses a frequency band where one term dominates. This section separates that term and gives a bound for the other terms.

6.1Isolating one pole

Let \(s=j\omega\) on the imaginary axis, where \(\omega=2\pi f\). Separate mode \(m\):

\[H(j\omega)=\frac{R_m}{j\omega-\lambda_m}+E_m(j\omega),\qquad R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(6.1)

The remainder \(E_m\) contains the conjugate pole and all other modes. It is finite at \(\omega_{d,m}\) (A5). The selected denominator is

\[j\omega-\lambda_m=\alpha_m+j(\omega-\omega_{d,m}),\qquad |j\omega-\lambda_m|^2=\alpha_m^2+(\omega-\omega_{d,m})^2.\]

At \(\omega=\omega_{d,m}\), the magnitude of the denominator is \(\alpha_m\). Thus, the selected term grows as \(1/\alpha_m\). The remainder has no such denominator.

6.2The exact four-term expansion

Let \(P=R_m/(j\omega-\lambda_m)\) and \(E=E_m\). Substitute these terms into (5.3):

\[G_y=(P+E)G_x(P+E)^H=PG_xP^H+PG_xE^H+EG_xP^H+EG_xE^H.\]
(6.2)

This expansion is exact. The first term contains the selected pole twice. The two middle terms combine the selected pole with the remainder. The last term contains the remainder twice. Since \(P^H=R_m^H/(-j\omega-\bar\lambda_m)\),

\[PG_xP^H=\frac{R_mG_xR_m^H}{|j\omega-\lambda_m|^2}.\]

With \(R_m^H=\bar\gamma_mv_m^H\),

\[R_mG_xR_m^H=v_m\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m\,v_m^H=\left(\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m\right)v_mv_m^H.\]

Define the scalar weight

\[G_y\approx d_m\,v_mv_m^H,\qquad d_m(f)=\frac{\gamma_m^TG_x(f)\bar\gamma_m}{\alpha_m^2+(2\pi f-\omega_{d,m})^2}\ge0.\]
(6.3)

The numerator is \((\bar\gamma_m)^HG_x(\bar\gamma_m)\ge0\), because \(G_x\) is positive semidefinite. The direction \(v_m\) is constant. Only the weight \(d_m\) changes with frequency. If \(G_x\approx Q_x\) is constant,

\[d_m(f)=\frac{\gamma_m^TQ_x\bar\gamma_m}{\alpha_m^2+(2\pi f-\omega_{d,m})^2},\]

This is a Lorentzian curve with a peak at \(\omega_{d,m}\). The numerator sets its height. The decay rate \(\alpha_m\) sets its width.

6.3The residual bound

You can omit the last three terms of (6.2) when they are small. Weight the selected term and the remainder by the excitation. Let \(G_x^{1/2}\) be the positive-semidefinite square root of \(G_x\):

\[P_w=PG_x^{1/2},\qquad E_w=EG_x^{1/2}.\]

Then \(P_wP_w^H=PG_xP^H\), \(P_wE_w^H=PG_xE^H\), \(E_wP_w^H=EG_xP^H\), \(E_wE_w^H=EG_xE^H\), so

\[G_y=P_wP_w^H+P_wE_w^H+E_wP_w^H+E_wE_w^H.\]

With \(\|\cdot\|_2\) the spectral norm, the triangle inequality and \(\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2\) give

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le2\|P_w\|_2\|E_w\|_2+\|E_w\|_2^2.\]
(6.4)

If \(\|E_w\|_2\le\varepsilon\|P_w\|_2\), then

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)\|P_w\|_2^2.\]

The value of \(\varepsilon\) depends on the force spectrum, modal visibility, and pole separation. It does not depend only on damping. A lightly damped mode can have a large \(\varepsilon\) near a strongly excited mode. A heavily damped mode can have a small \(\varepsilon\) if it is alone in the band. Appendix C gives the bound again.

6.4What the assumption really says

The assumption applies to the response in the selected band. In that band, the selected mode must dominate the response that the forces excite and the sensors measure. A mode can be invisible if \(v_m\) is small, for example when the sensors are near a node. It can also be invisible if \(\gamma_m\) is small, because the forces do not excite it sufficiently. Close modes increase \(E\). A colored force spectrum can distort the weight and shift the peak. Correlated inputs matter when more than one mode contributes. Noise adds a full-rank term.

6.5Worked example: two sensors

Assume that one mode moves sensor 1 by \(1\) mm and sensor 2 by \(-2\) mm. Let \(a(t)\) be the first displacement:

\[y(t)=\begin{bmatrix}a(t)\\-2a(t)\end{bmatrix}=v\,a(t),\qquad v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}.\]
(6.5)

Let \(A(f)\) be the Fourier coefficient of \(a\). By linearity, \(Y_1=A\) and \(Y_2=-2A\). Thus,

\[|Y_1|^2=|A|^2,\qquad |Y_2|^2=4|A|^2,\qquad Y_1\bar Y_2=-2|A|^2.\]

The separate power spectra do not show the sign. The cross product shows that the sensors move in opposite directions. Average and normalize the products:

\[G_y(f)=S_a(f)\,vv^T=S_a(f)\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\-2&4\end{bmatrix},\]
(6.6)

where \(S_a\) is the PSD of \(a\). For \(S_a>0\) the matrix is rank one, and

\[G_yv=5S_a(f)\,v,\qquad G_y\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=0.\]
(6.7)

The eigenvalue depends on excitation strength. The eigenvector gives the relative motion \(1:-2\). The null vector \([2,1]^T\) is orthogonal to \(v\). The shape has an arbitrary scale and phase. Where \(S_a=0\), there is no preferred direction. FDD therefore estimates shapes at peaks.

7Frequency domain decomposition

FDD diagonalizes the measured spectral matrix at each frequency. This decomposition is exact. The interpretation of the leading vector as a mode shape uses the approximation in §6.

7.1The SVD at each frequency

At frequency \(f_i\), diagonalize the estimate \(\widehat G_y\):

\[\widehat G_y(f_i)=U_iS_iU_i^H=\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_k(f_i)\,u_k(f_i)u_k(f_i)^H,\qquad s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge s_p\ge0.\]
(7.1)

The columns of \(U_i\) are orthonormal eigenvectors. The matrix \(S_i\) is diagonal. For a Hermitian positive-semidefinite matrix, the SVD and eigendecomposition give the same result. The singular values are the eigenvalues. The left singular vectors are the eigenvectors. The first term is the largest.

An exact rank-one matrix has only \(s_1\ne0\). Other modes, noise, and finite-record errors usually make the estimate full rank. For \(u_1\) to be close to the physical shape, \(s_2\) must be sufficiently small. See Appendix E.

7.2The leading singular vector

For \(G\approx d\,vv^H\) with \(v\ne0\),

\[\left(d\,vv^H\right)v=d\,\|v\|^2\,v,\]
(7.2)

so \(v\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(d\|v\|^2\). Normalizing,

\[u_1\approx e^{j\theta}\frac{v}{\|v\|},\qquad s_1\approx d\,\|v\|^2.\]
(7.3)

The phase \(\theta\) is arbitrary. You can multiply \(v\) by a constant and adjust \(d\) without changing \(d\,vv^H\). The absolute scale and phase are therefore unknown. Modal mass is not identifiable from this result. For \(G=G_0+E\), with \(G_0=d\,vv^H\), the leading vector is close to \(v/\|v\|\) when \(\|E\|_2\) is small relative to the spectral gap. See the bound in Appendix E. A large \(s_1/s_2\) supports a well-defined direction. A ratio near one does not. For the two-sensor example, \(u_1=[1,-2]^T/\sqrt5\) and \(s_1=5S_a\).

7.3Peak frequency is not pole frequency

The spectral peak of a single oscillator can differ from \(\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d\). Omit the subscripts. The force-to-displacement transfer function is

\[h(j\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+j2\zeta\omega_n\omega},\]

so

\[\left|h(j\omega)\right|^2=\frac{1}{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+4\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega^2}.\]
(7.4)

Under white forcing the displacement PSD has the same frequency dependence. Minimize the denominator \(D(\omega)\):

\[D'(\omega)=2(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)(-2\omega)+8\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega=4\omega\left[\omega^2-\omega_n^2+2\zeta^2\omega_n^2\right].\]

The stationary points are \(\omega=0\) and \(\omega^2=\omega_n^2(1-2\zeta^2)\). If \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2\), the positive root gives a minimum of \(D\) and a maximum of \(|h|^2\). If \(\zeta\ge1/\sqrt2\), the maximum occurs at \(\omega=0\). Thus,

\[\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}\quad(\zeta<1/\sqrt2),\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=0\quad(\zeta\ge1/\sqrt2).\]
(7.5)

For light damping, the three frequencies are close. For \(0<\zeta<1/\sqrt2\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}<\omega_d<\omega_n\). Colored forcing, acceleration measurement, and finite resolution can shift the measured peak. An acceleration PSD has an additional factor of \(\omega^4\), which biases the peak upward.

7.4Two modes in one band

For two modes,

\[G_y=d_1v_1v_1^H+d_2v_2v_2^H.\]
(7.6)

If \(v_1^Hv_2=0\), the eigenvectors are exactly \(v_1,v_2\), with eigenvalues \(d_1\|v_1\|^2,d_2\|v_2\|^2\). Otherwise

\[G_yv_1=d_1\|v_1\|^2v_1+d_2v_2\left(v_2^Hv_1\right),\]
(7.7)

The second term points away from \(v_1\). Thus, \(v_1\) is not an eigenvector and the leading vector is a mixture. If \(d_1,d_2>0\) and \(v_1,v_2\) are independent, \(G_y\) maps into their two-dimensional span. The first two singular vectors recover this span, but not the individual shapes. Orthogonal measured shapes are a special case. Mass-orthogonal physical modes do not necessarily give orthogonal measured shapes, because \(L\) does not preserve the mass inner product. If the singular values are equal, the basis within the subspace is not unique.

The modal assurance criterion measures agreement:

\[\operatorname{MAC}(u,v)=\frac{|u^Hv|^2}{(u^Hu)(v^Hv)}\in[0,1].\]
(7.8)

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives a value between zero and one. The value is one for proportional vectors and zero for orthogonal vectors. It does not change when you scale either vector by a nonzero complex number. Use MAC to compare directions at adjacent frequencies.

7.5The algorithm

FDD, step by step

  1. Acquire. Record \(p\) synchronized channels with known units and orientations; choose segment length, window, and overlap.
  2. Estimate. Compute \(\widehat G_y(f_i)\) for every bin, using the same segments and window for all channel pairs and enough independent segments.
  3. Decompose. Diagonalize at each \(f_i\) (7.1); store the singular values and leading vectors.
  4. Find peaks. Plot \(s_1(f)\); a local maximum is a candidate resonance.
  5. Read the shape. At a peak take \(u_1\); check that \(s_1/s_2\) is large and \(u_1\) is stable across bins by MAC.
  6. Estimate damping. If needed, select the bell around the peak by MAC and fit its correlation decay (§8).

Output. A candidate peak frequency and a shape \(u_1\), with arbitrary scale and phase. EFDD can also estimate the damped frequency \(f_{d,m}\) and damping ratio \(\zeta_m\).

The eigendecomposition costs \(O(N_fp^3)\) for \(N_f\) bins and \(p\) channels. This cost is small when \(p\) is small. The spectral estimate costs \(O(N\log N)\) per segment. Sensor placement and spectral settings require careful selection.

8Estimating damping: EFDD

FDD gives an estimate of shape and peak frequency. EFDD uses the width of the spectral peak to estimate damping. Select the part of the peak that belongs to one mode. Then inverse-transform it and fit the decay of its correlation.

8.1Selecting the spectral bell

Use the leading vector near a peak as the reference \(\hat v_m\). At each adjacent frequency, calculate \(\operatorname{MAC}(u_k(f),\hat v_m)\). Keep a bin if its MAC exceeds the threshold. The selected bins form the spectral bell. A threshold of \(0.8\) is a common starting point. Track the direction by MAC. Do not rely on vector index, because the first and second singular vectors can cross. Set the rejected bins to zero. Examine the bell for asymmetry or roughness, which can indicate contamination.

The selected spectrum \(b^+(f)\) is one-sided. Its value at \(k\Delta f\) is \(b^+[k]\). Appendix D defines the \(^+\) scaling. Double the interior bins, but do not double DC or Nyquist.

8.2From the bell to a correlation decay

The Wiener–Khinchin relation gives autocorrelation as the inverse transform of the PSD. For a real, even correlation, the result is a cosine integral:

\[\mathcal R_m(\tau)=\int_0^\infty b^+(f)\cos(2\pi f\tau)\,df\approx\Delta f\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}b^+[k]\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k\ell}{N}\right),\qquad \tau=\frac{\ell}{f_s}.\]
(8.1)

Here \(f_s\) is the sampling rate, \(N\) is an even transform length, \(\Delta f=f_s/N\), and \(\ell\) is the lag index. The cosine comes from the mirrored negative frequencies. For a single mode, the correlation oscillates with the envelope \(a_m(\tau)=A_0e^{-\alpha_m\tau}\), where \(A_0>0\). Its period is \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_{d,m}\):

\[\mathcal R_m(\tau)\propto a_m(\tau)\cos(\omega_{d,m}\tau),\qquad T_d=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{d,m}}.\]

8.3From log decrement to damping ratio

At two times separated by \(k\) periods, the envelope ratio is \(e^{-\alpha_mkT_d}\). The logarithm of this ratio per period is the logarithmic decrement:

\[\delta=\frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{a_m(\tau)}{a_m(\tau+kT_d)}=\alpha_mT_d.\]
(8.2)

Substitute \(\alpha_m=\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}\) and \(T_d=2\pi/(\omega_{n,m}\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2})\); the natural frequency cancels:

\[\delta=\frac{2\pi\zeta_m}{\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}}.\]

Square and solve for \(\zeta_m\):

\[\delta^2(1-\zeta_m^2)=4\pi^2\zeta_m^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\zeta_m^2=\frac{\delta^2}{4\pi^2+\delta^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\zeta_m=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}},\]

taking the positive root. Then

\[\zeta_m=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}},\qquad f_{n,m}=\frac{f_{d,m}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}}.\]
(8.3)

Fit a line to several peaks: \(\ln a_m(\tau_j)=\ln A_0-\alpha_m\tau_j\). The slope gives \(\alpha_m\). Peaks with the same sign are one period apart. Alternating extrema are half a period apart. Exclude the initial distorted part and the late noisy part.

8.4Practical bias

A nearby mode produces beating and can bias a single-exponential fit. A bell that is too narrow truncates the peak. A bell that is too wide includes neighboring modes and noise. Leakage changes the shape of the bell. Nonstationary excitation changes its height. Calculate damping for different bell widths and fitting intervals. A result that changes can indicate that the bell does not isolate one mode. Compare the EFDD frequency with (3.7) before you report it.

Part IVPractice

9Using the method on measurements

The checks below help you decide if the results are reliable.

9.1Before the measurement

  • Sensor placement. A sensor at a node records none of that mode. Place sensors where the target modes are large, and use enough to distinguish the shapes; \(v_m=L\phi_m\) is limited by \(L\).
  • Units and directions. Record the measured quantity, units, and positive direction. Arbitrary rescaling changes the geometry seen by the SVD; apply rotations before the spectral estimate.
  • Synchronization. All channels need one time base. A constant offset adds a frequency-dependent phase shift and corrupts the cross-spectra.
  • Stationarity. The excitation must be stationary over the record or each segment; transients, gusts, and operating changes violate A6.

9.2During spectral estimation

  • Same segments and window. Use them for every entry of \(\widehat G_y\); otherwise the cross-channel phase is destroyed.
  • Enough averages. One segment gives a noisy estimate with artificially low rank. Overlap adds segments but not independent averages.
  • Resolution. Segment length sets resolution; zero-padding only densifies the grid. A peak narrower than the resolution cannot be separated from its neighbor.
  • Check stability. Plot \(s_1(f)\) and \(s_1/s_2\); a peak that moves with segment length or averaging is not stable.

9.3Interpreting the decomposition

  • Stable direction. The leading vector should be stable by MAC across the peak; rapid rotation means a mixture.
  • Rank one. A large \(s_1/s_2\) supports the interpretation; a ratio near one leaves the direction undetermined.
  • Harmonic forcing. A sinusoid also gives a narrow rank-one peak. Check whether the frequency matches a known forcing frequency and whether the shape is structural.
  • Visibility. An absent peak does not prove an absent mode; it may be weakly excited or invisible at the sensors.
  • Nonlinearity. Harmonics, combination frequencies, and amplitude-dependent shapes violate the linear model.

9.4Reporting

Report the sensor coordinates, directions, measured quantity, and units. Report the shape normalization, phase convention, and cross-spectrum convention. Give the segment length, window, overlap, number of averages, and resolution. For EFDD, also report the bell, MAC threshold, and fit interval. State whether each frequency is damped \(f_d\) or natural \(f_n\). These frequencies differ by \(\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). If a check fails, report the mixture or possible bias. Do not present the leading vector as a mode shape without qualification.

AState-space linearization

The quadratic eigenvalue problem (3.2) has an equivalent ordinary eigenproblem. The state vector includes displacement and velocity. Let \(z\) be the \(2n\)-component state vector. Let \(I\) be the \(n\times n\) identity matrix. The zero blocks have the same size. The free equation \(M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=0\) becomes

\[z=\begin{bmatrix}q\\\dot q\end{bmatrix},\qquad \dot z=Az,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\-M^{-1}K&-M^{-1}C\end{bmatrix}.\]
(A.1)

The upper block of \(\dot z=Az\) gives the identity \(\dot q=\dot q\). The lower block gives \(\ddot q=-M^{-1}Kq-M^{-1}C\dot q\). This is the free equation multiplied by \(M^{-1}\). The matrix \(A\) is \(2n\times2n\). Thus, it has \(2n\) eigenvalues, with repeated roots counted by multiplicity. These are the eigenvalues of the quadratic problem, as the next calculation shows.

Let \(z=\Psi e^{\lambda t}\) be a state solution. Then \(\dot z=\lambda\Psi e^{\lambda t}\). Cancellation of the exponential gives \(A\Psi=\lambda\Psi\). Let \(\Psi_1\) and \(\Psi_2\) be the upper and lower \(n\)-component blocks of \(\Psi\). The two block equations are

\[\lambda\Psi_1=\Psi_2,\qquad -M^{-1}K\Psi_1-M^{-1}C\Psi_2=\lambda\Psi_2.\]
(A.2)

The first equation gives \(\Psi_2=\lambda\Psi_1\). Substitute into the second and multiply by \(M\):

\[-K\Psi_1-\lambda C\Psi_1=\lambda^2M\Psi_1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda C+K\right)\Psi_1=0.\]

Thus, \(\Psi_1\) is a null vector of \(Z(\lambda)\). It is a mode shape \(\phi_m\), and the state eigenvector is

\[\Psi_m=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix},\qquad Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0.\]

Each nonzero solution of the quadratic problem also gives a state eigenvector. This vector has \(\phi_m\) above \(\lambda_m\phi_m\). The correspondence is exact:

\[Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix}=\lambda_m\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix}.\]

In the quadratic problem, \(\lambda_m\) selects the matrix \(Z(\lambda_m)\). The vector \(\phi_m\) is in its nullspace. It does not satisfy \(Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=\lambda_m\phi_m\). The first \(n\) entries of the state eigenvector give the displacement mode shape.

The two problems have the same \(2n\) eigenvalues, with repeated roots counted by multiplicity. Real matrices give conjugate pairs of nonreal roots. A defective eigenvalue has fewer independent eigenvectors than its multiplicity. Its time response also requires polynomial factors. The simple-pole expansion on this page excludes that case.

The state form also shows why the exponential ansatz is sufficient. The solution of \(\dot z=Az\) is a matrix exponential. It is a combination of \(e^{\lambda t}\) terms, with polynomial factors for defective eigenvalues.

A.1Damped modal parameters and undamped structural frequencies

The phrase “remove the damping” has two meanings. For the equivalent scalar oscillator, we set its damping ratio to zero. We keep the frequency parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\). For the whole structure, we set \(C=0\) in (2.7) and solve a new eigenproblem. Let \(\Omega_r\) be an undamped angular frequency and \(\psi_r\) its displacement shape. Substitution of \(q=\psi e^{j\Omega t}\) into the undamped equation gives

\[M\ddot q+Kq=0,\qquad K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r.\]
(A.3)

This is a generalized symmetric eigenproblem. The matrix \(M^{-1}K\) is not symmetric in the ordinary inner product. It is symmetric in the \(M\)-inner product. Its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative when \(M\succ0\) and \(K\succeq0\). For one mass and spring, (A.3) and the scalar oscillator both give \(\sqrt{k/\mu}\).

The two frequencies also agree mode by mode for proportional damping, \(C=aM+bK\), where \(a,b\) are constants. Apply \(Z(\lambda)\) to an undamped mode \(\psi_r\). Then use \(K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r\):

\[Z(\lambda)\psi_r=\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda aM+\lambda bK+K\right)\psi_r=\left[\lambda^2+a\lambda+\Omega_r^2(1+b\lambda)\right]M\psi_r.\]
(A.4)

Since \(M\psi_r\ne0\), the bracket must vanish. It is a quadratic in \(\lambda\),

\[\lambda^2+(a+b\Omega_r^2)\lambda+\Omega_r^2=0.\]

Comparison with \(\lambda^2+2\zeta_r\Omega_r\lambda+\Omega_r^2\) gives \(\omega_{n,r}=\Omega_r\) and \(\zeta_r=(a/\Omega_r+b\Omega_r)/2\). The damped mode has the undamped shape with an added decay. The two definitions of natural frequency agree.

For general damping, the damped modes can combine several undamped shapes. Also, \(|\lambda_m|\) is not necessarily equal to an \(\Omega_r\). On this page, “natural frequency of mode \(m\)” means the equivalent-oscillator parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\).

BThe residue formula via the adjugate

Section 4.4 shows that the residue is rank one. It gives the input-coupling vector \(\gamma_m\) as a limit. This appendix evaluates that limit. The result gives a formula for implementations. It also shows when a mode is absent from the transfer matrix.

A cofactor belongs to row \(i\) and column \(j\) of a square matrix. Remove that row and column, then calculate the determinant of the remaining matrix. The cofactor is \((-1)^{i+j}\) times that determinant. The adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}Z\) is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. It is also defined for singular matrices and satisfies

\[Z\operatorname{adj}Z=\operatorname{adj}Z\,Z=(\det Z)I.\]
(B.1)

Wherever \(Z\) is invertible, \(Z^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}Z/\det Z\). The transfer matrix is therefore

\[H(s)=L\frac{\operatorname{adj}Z(s)}{\det Z(s)}B.\]

At a simple root \(\lambda_m\), the determinant has a simple zero. The adjugate has rank one. To see this, use (B.1). At the root, \(\det Z=0\), so \(Z\operatorname{adj}Z=0\). Each column of the adjugate is in the one-dimensional nullspace of \(Z(\lambda_m)\). The adjugate is not zero because the root is simple. Thus,

\[\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)=c_m\,\phi_m\ell_m^H,\]
(B.2)

where \(c_m\ne0\) is a constant. The right null vector \(\phi_m\) satisfies \(Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0\). The left null vector \(\ell_m^H\) satisfies \(\ell_m^HZ(\lambda_m)=0\). Both null vectors are nonzero because the root is simple.

Near the root, expand the determinant in a Taylor series. Let \(d'_m\) be its derivative at \(\lambda_m\). Then

\[\det Z(s)=(s-\lambda_m)d'_m+O\!\left((s-\lambda_m)^2\right).\]
(B.3)

Near the root, the remainder \(O((s-\lambda_m)^2)\) is bounded by a constant times \(|s-\lambda_m|^2\). Jacobi’s identity gives the derivative. The identity is also valid for singular matrices:

\[d'_m=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)Z'(\lambda_m)\right),\qquad Z'(s)=2sM+C.\]

Substitution of (B.2) into the trace gives \(d'_m=c_m\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m\). In the residue limit, the factors \(s-\lambda_m\) cancel. The constants \(c_m\) also cancel:

\[R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s)=\frac{L\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)B}{d'_m}=\frac{(L\phi_m)(\ell_m^HB)}{\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m}=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(B.4)

Thus the input-coupling vector is

\[\gamma_m^T=\frac{\ell_m^HB}{\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m}.\]

The denominator is nonzero at a simple root. If it were zero, the determinant derivative would be zero and the root would not be simple. The residue is zero if \(L\phi_m=0\), so the mode is unobserved. It is also zero if \(\ell_m^HB=0\), so the mode is unexcited.

In a minimal state realization, each state direction is reachable from the inputs and observable in the outputs. Thus, the transfer matrix retains every state pole. Individual entries can still omit modes.

Let \(H_{\dot y}\) and \(H_{\ddot y}\) be the force-to-velocity and force-to-acceleration transfer matrices. With zero initial conditions, a time derivative gives a factor of \(s\). Thus, \(H_{\dot y}=sH\) and \(H_{\ddot y}=s^2H\). The high-frequency limit of \(H_{\ddot y}\) is the feedthrough \(LM^{-1}B\). Equivalently, \(H_{\ddot y}=LM^{-1}B+\tilde H(s)\), where \(\tilde H(s)\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\).

The velocity and acceleration residues have factors \(\lambda_m\) and \(\lambda_m^2\), respectively. The feedthrough adds a constant term with no pole. Real or repeated roots require a different expansion. If the modal sum includes only a few modes, it has a residual.

Inverse expansion: [1], §3.5, equation (3.11). Factorized residues: [3], equations (2)–(3). The adjugate calculation supplies the intermediate steps.

CA bound on the omitted response

This appendix repeats the bound in §6.3 with the same notation. Assume that the force spectral matrix \(G_x\) is constant across the band. Let \(Q_x\) denote this matrix. Let \(Q_x^{1/2}\) be its Hermitian positive-semidefinite square root. Define the selected term and remainder, weighted by the excitation:

\[P=\frac{v_m\gamma_m^T}{j\omega-\lambda_m},\qquad E=E_m(j\omega),\qquad P_w=PQ_x^{1/2},\qquad E_w=EQ_x^{1/2}.\]
(C.1)

Expansion of (5.3) gives

\[G_y=P_wP_w^H+P_wE_w^H+E_wP_w^H+E_wE_w^H.\]

Let \(\|\cdot\|_2\) be the matrix spectral norm. The triangle inequality and the submultiplicative property \(\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2\) give

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le2\|P_w\|_2\|E_w\|_2+\|E_w\|_2^2.\]

If \(\|E_w\|_2\le\varepsilon\|P_w\|_2\), then

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)\|P_w\|_2^2,\qquad \|P_w\|_2^2=\left\|P_wP_w^H\right\|_2.\]
(C.2)

The second equality relates the bound to the size of the selected term. The condition on \(\varepsilon\) is sufficient for an accurate rank-one approximation. It is not necessary. A matrix can be close to rank one even if this bound is loose.

The bound depends on the excitation. For a small \(\varepsilon\), the weighted omitted response must be small relative to the weighted selected response. Light damping alone does not ensure this condition.

DSpectral conventions and estimation

The main text uses one cross-spectrum convention and one spectral-density normalization. This appendix states both. It derives the normalization and describes effects on shape and damping estimates.

D.1Conjugation order

Some sources define the cross-spectral matrix with \(\bar YY^T\) instead of \(YY^H\). Let \(\widetilde G_y\) denote that matrix. Since \(Y^H=\bar Y^T\),

\[\widetilde G_y=\bar YY^T=G_y^T=\bar G_y.\]
(D.1)

With this convention, the rank-one term is \(\widetilde G_y\approx d_m\bar v_mv_m^T\). The leading vector estimates \(\bar v_m\). Its conjugate gives the shape convention on this page. Both conventions are valid, but one calculation must use the same convention throughout.

SciPy’s csd(a, b) uses conjugate-first order. If y[i] contains channel \(i\), csd(y[j], y[i]) gives entry \((i,j)\) of this page’s matrix. [6]

D.2Welch’s estimate

Welch’s method divides a record into segments. The segments can overlap. The method applies the same window to each channel and averages the spectral products. Detrending removes a fitted mean or trend. A window gives each sample a real weight. This controls spectral spreading from a finite record.

Let \(N_b\) be the number of segments and \(N_w\) their length in samples. Let \(w[\nu]\) be the window at index \(\nu\) within a segment. The indices \(b\) and \(k\) identify segments and frequency bins. Let \(Y_b[k]\) be the vector of unnormalized discrete Fourier transform (DFT) coefficients after detrending and windowing. A fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the DFT.

The one-sided factor \(\eta_k\) is \(2\) at interior positive-frequency bins. It is \(1\) at DC and at Nyquist, when present. At sampling rate \(f_s\), the estimate is

\[\widehat G_y^+[k]=\frac{\eta_k}{N_b f_s\sum_{\nu=0}^{N_w-1}w[\nu]^2}\sum_{b=1}^{N_b}Y_b[k]Y_b[k]^H.\]
(D.2)

A white-noise example shows the normalization. Assume a white channel with variance \(\sigma^2\) and sampling rate \(f_s\). Its two-sided PSD is constant: \(G=\sigma^2/f_s\). The integral over the Nyquist band \([-f_s/2,f_s/2]\) is \(Gf_s=\sigma^2\). For one segment, the expected squared magnitude of the windowed DFT coefficient is \(\mathbb E|Y_b[k]|^2=\sigma^2\sum_\nu w[\nu]^2\). Substitute \(G=\sigma^2/f_s\):

\[\mathbb E|Y_b[k]|^2=G\,f_s\sum_{\nu=0}^{N_w-1}w[\nu]^2.\]

Division by \(f_s\sum w^2\) therefore gives an unbiased estimate of \(G\). The average over \(N_b\) segments reduces its variance. The factor \(\eta_k\) converts the two-sided density to the one-sided convention. For a colored signal, the window convolves the spectrum. Thus, the estimate is a smoothed version of the true density. The normalization preserves the integral, but not the exact shape.

Identical segments and windows preserve the Hermitian positive-semidefinite structure. Each segment contributes at most one independent direction to the average. Thus, the estimated rank cannot exceed \(N_b\). This limit comes from finite data and is not a physical rank.

The average reduces variability between estimates. It does not remove a persistent noise or forcing spectrum. Longer segments improve frequency resolution. More effectively independent segments improve the average. Overlap does not make segments independent.

Zeros added before the DFT give more samples of the same windowed spectrum. They do not improve the resolution set by the segment duration and window. [5]

D.3Density units and the inverse transform

The spectra on this page are densities per hertz. The superscripts \((f)\) and \((\omega)\) identify densities per hertz and per radian per second. The change of variables \(d\omega=2\pi\,df\) gives

\[G^{(\omega)}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}G^{(f)}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right).\]
(D.3)

For the EFDD inverse transform, mirror the one-sided spectrum to negative frequencies before the inverse DFT. Halve the one-sided interior positive bins. Copy them to the corresponding negative bins. Keep the DC and Nyquist endpoint weights from (D.2).

The inverse DFT has a normalization of \(1/N\). The frequency spacing is \(\Delta f=f_s/N\). The inverse transform sums \(\Delta f\,G[k]e^{j2\pi k\ell/N}\). Thus, the correlation is \(f_s\) times the inverse DFT of the mirrored spectrum.

Normalization by \(\mathcal R_m(0)\) removes an overall scale. It does not correct incorrect mirroring or endpoint weights. Incorrect one-sided scaling can bias the damping estimate. [5]

D.4Additive sensor noise

Let \(\eta(t)\) denote sensor noise and \(G_\eta\) its PSD. If the noise and structural response have zero cross-covariance at every lag, their spectra add:

\[G_{y+\eta}=G_y+G_\eta.\]
(D.4)

Correlated noise adds cross terms. Thus, a large singular value can also come from the measurement process. Noise raises all singular values. It has a proportionally larger effect on the smaller values. Thus, it reduces the ratio \(s_1/s_2\) used for the rank-one interpretation.

EA perturbation bound for the leading singular vector

Section 7.2 states that a small perturbation gives a leading singular vector close to the physical shape. This appendix gives a bound for that statement. The Davis–Kahan theorem gives a sharper form that uses the spectral gap. [11]

Let \(G=G_0+E\) be Hermitian. Let \(G_0=d\,vv^H\), \(\|v\|=1\), and \(d>0\). Let \(u\) be a unit eigenvector of \(G\) for its largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\). Assume that \(\lambda_1>0\). Divide \(u\) into a component along \(v\) and a component orthogonal to \(v\):

\[u=\alpha v+\beta w,\qquad w\perp v,\qquad \|w\|=1,\qquad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.\]

The angle \(\theta\) between \(u\) and \(v\) satisfies \(\sin\theta=|\beta|\). Because \(u\) is an eigenvector, \(Gu=\lambda_1u\). Take the inner product with \(w\):

\[\lambda_1\beta=w^HGu=w^H(G_0+E)u.\]

Because \(w\perp v\), the unperturbed term is zero: \(w^HG_0u=d\,w^Hv(v^Hu)=0\). Thus, \(\lambda_1\beta=w^HEu\). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\[|\beta|\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1},\qquad \sin\theta\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1}.\]
(E.1)

This bound relates the angular error to the perturbation relative to the leading eigenvalue. When \(\lambda_1\) is approximately \(d\), \(s_1\) estimates \(\lambda_1\). A large \(s_1\) relative to the perturbation is favorable.

A sharper form replaces \(\lambda_1\) by the gap between the first and second eigenvalues. If the leading eigenvalue is simple and the perturbation does not close the gap, the Davis–Kahan theorem gives

\[\sin\theta\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}.\]

When the second eigenvalue is close to the first, the denominator is small and the bound is weak. Thus, a ratio \(s_1/s_2\) near one leaves the direction undetermined. A large gap can make the direction stable even if the absolute perturbation is not very small. The bound is sufficient, not necessary. A direction can be stable for reasons that this inequality does not capture.
