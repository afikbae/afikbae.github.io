Frequency domain decomposition

Output-only modal analysis asks what a structure is doing when only its vibration is measured. This page derives frequency domain decomposition from first principles: energy, the equation of motion, eigenvalues, transfer functions, power spectra, and the singular value decomposition. It then derives enhanced FDD for damping. Every approximation is stated where it enters, together with the assumption that licenses it.

FDD answers three practical questions. Which frequencies does the structure prefer? What relative motion do its points have at those frequencies? How much damping does each of those motions have? The first two are answered by decomposing a measured spectral matrix at each frequency. The third is answered by transforming a selected part of that spectrum back into a decaying correlation. The derivation is longer than the recipe, because the recipe is only valid under conditions that the derivation makes explicit.

Assumptions we use

The derivation is exact under the first group of assumptions and approximate under the last two. Each assumption is numbered so that later sections can refer to it, and each is revisited in §9 where its failure is discussed. If you want the short version: the structure is linear and viscously damped, its eigenvalues are simple and stable, the excitation is stationary and random, the sensors are synchronized and calibrated, and near a resonance one mode dominates the measured response.

Model

A0
Output-only. Only the outputs \(y(t)\) are measured. The inputs \(x(t)\) are unknown and are modeled as a random process. No measured force is available to normalize the result.
A1
Linear, time-invariant, finite-dimensional. The structure has \(n\) displacement coordinates, \(r\) force inputs, and \(p\) sensors. Displacements are small about a static equilibrium, so \(M,C,K\) are constant matrices.
A2
Viscous damping. Damping is represented by a constant matrix \(C\). General damping can make the modes complex; the residue derivation still holds, but a “mode” is then a complex mode, not a real standing wave.
A3
Linear input and output maps. Forces enter as \(Bx\) and sensors read \(Lq\). There are no sensor dynamics, time delays, saturation, or nonlinearities. Sensor gains and directions are absorbed into \(L\).
A4
Matrix properties. \(M=M^T\succ0\), \(K=K^T\succeq0\), and \(C=C^T\succeq0\). If the structure is restrained, \(K\succ0\); a free-free structure has rigid-body modes with \(K\phi=0\). These properties follow from energy in §2.4.
A5
Simple, stable eigenvalues. The \(2n\) eigenvalues of the quadratic pencil are distinct, have negative real part, and occur in conjugate pairs. Every pole is simple. Repeated or defective roots need a different expansion and are excluded here.

Excitation and measurement

A6
Stationary random excitation. \(x(t)\) is zero-mean and wide-sense stationary, with finite variance and a spectral density matrix in the band of interest. Ergodicity lets a time average over records estimate the ensemble average.
A7
Accurate estimate. The estimated spectral matrix \(\widehat G_y\) is close to the true \(G_y\). Finite-record bias, variance, leakage, and resolution are treated separately in Appendix D.
A8
Clean, synchronized measurements. Measurement noise is zero or uncorrelated with the response. Channels are synchronized, calibrated, and share a known orientation and units.
A9
One dominant mode in the band. Across the band used for a mode, the force spectral matrix is approximately constant, \(G_x(f)\approx Q_x\), and the selected mode dominates the excited response: the residual \(E_m\) is small relative to the selected term. This is the FDD approximation, made precise in §6.
A10
One mode in the bell. For EFDD, the selected spectral bell contains one mode and its correlation decays as a single exponential. Close modes, beating, or a truncated bell violate this assumption.

Conventions

A11
Fourier and spectral conventions. \(j^2=-1\). The Fourier transform uses \(e^{-j2\pi ft}\). The spectral matrix \(G_y\) is two-sided unless marked \(^+\); for a one-sided spectrum, interior positive-frequency bins are doubled and the DC and Nyquist bins are not. \(f\) is in hertz and \(\omega=2\pi f\) is in radians per second. A displacement PSD has units of displacement squared per hertz.
A12
Scale and phase are not identified. The measured shape is determined only up to a complex scale. Output-only data do not determine modal mass or an absolute shape normalization. Only relative amplitudes and phases are meaningful.

The derivation is exact under A0–A6 and A8. A7 is an estimation assumption. A9 is the approximation that makes FDD work, and A10 is the approximation that makes EFDD work. When either fails, the method does not become invalid; it returns a mixture, a biased estimate, or a subspace instead of one shape, as §7.4 and §9 explain.

Part IThe model

1The measurement problem

A structure is vibrating. We attach sensors at a few points, record their motion for a while, and never measure the forces that caused the motion. From those records we want three things: the frequencies at which the structure prefers to vibrate, the relative motion of the sensor points at each such frequency, and how quickly that motion dies away. The first two are the frequencies and shapes of the structure’s modes. The third is their damping.

A mode shape is a fixed pattern of relative amplitudes and phases. If a single mode were the only motion present, every sensor record would be a scalar multiple of one fixed vector, with the same time dependence up to that scalar. Two sensors might move in the same direction with amplitudes in the ratio \(1:2\), or in opposite directions with a phase difference of \(180^\circ\). The ratio and the phase difference would not change with time. That fixed pattern is the shape.

Real records contain several modes at once, so the instantaneous ratio between two sensors usually changes with time. The shape is therefore not visible by inspecting the time histories directly. It becomes visible in a narrow frequency band around a resonance, where one mode may dominate the response. FDD looks for a fixed spatial pattern within a frequency band, and it uses the fact that a single dominant mode produces a spectral matrix of rank one.

The method is called output-only because the inputs are not measured. Experimental modal analysis with measured inputs can normalize the shape by the input force. Output-only analysis cannot: it recovers a shape only up to an arbitrary complex scale. That limitation is not an accident of the algorithm; it follows from the structure of the equations, as §6 shows.

There are two stages. The first estimates the cross-spectral density matrix of the sensor channels at each frequency and decomposes it. The dominant direction at a peak is a shape estimate. The second stage selects a band around a peak, transforms that band back to the time domain, and fits the decay of its correlation. That decay gives the damping. The first stage is FDD; the second is EFDD.

2Energy and the equation of motion

Before we can decompose a spectrum, we need a model of what the spectrum is a spectrum of. This section derives the equation of motion from energy. The derivation is worth doing in full because the properties that FDD relies on, namely symmetry and positive semidefiniteness, are properties of the energy, not of an arbitrary matrix.

2.1Energy of a vibrating structure

Let \(q(t)\in\mathbb R^n\) collect the displacement coordinates, measured from static equilibrium. For a linear structure, the kinetic energy is a quadratic form in the velocities, the strain energy is a quadratic form in the displacements, and the energy removed by viscous damping is a quadratic form in the velocities:

\[T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q,\qquad V=\tfrac12q^TKq,\qquad D=\tfrac12\dot q^TC\dot q.\]
(2.1)

Here \(M\), \(K\), and \(C\) are the mass, stiffness, and viscous damping matrices. The external forces do work through a virtual displacement \(\delta q\). If the forces are linear in the \(r\) inputs \(x(t)\), that work is

\[\delta W=(Bx)^T\delta q=x^TB^T\delta q.\]

The matrix \(B\), of size \(n\times r\), distributes each input to the coordinates. The vector \(Bx\) is the generalized force vector. The measured outputs are a linear map of the coordinates,

\[y=Lq,\]

with \(L\) of size \(p\times n\). Each row of \(L\) combines the coordinates into one sensor reading.

The quadratic forms are not an arbitrary modelling choice. Expand the true energy in a Taylor series about equilibrium. The linear term vanishes because equilibrium is a stationary point of the potential. The constant term can be taken as zero. For small displacements, the quadratic term dominates, and every higher-order term is negligible. That is the content of assumption A1. The matrices are therefore the second derivatives of the energy at equilibrium, which is why they are symmetric.

2.2One degree of freedom

Start with a mass \(\mu>0\), a spring \(k>0\), a viscous damper \(b\ge0\), and a force \(F(t)\). Let \(u(t)\) be the displacement from equilibrium. The three energies and the virtual work are

\[T=\tfrac12\mu\dot u^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12ku^2,\qquad D=\tfrac12b\dot u^2,\qquad \delta W=F\,\delta u.\]
(2.2)

The energy balance says that the rate of change of stored energy equals the power supplied by the force minus the power dissipated by the damper:

\[\frac{d}{dt}(T+V)=F\dot u-b\dot u^2.\]
(2.3)

Differentiate the left side:

\[\mu\dot u\ddot u+ku\dot u=F\dot u-b\dot u^2.\]

Bring every term to one side and factor out \(\dot u\):

\[\dot u\bigl(\mu\ddot u+b\dot u+ku-F\bigr)=0.\]

At any instant where \(\dot u\ne0\), we may divide by \(\dot u\). At an instant where \(\dot u=0\), the bracket need not be zero from this equation alone, but the acceleration and the force are continuous functions of time for the systems considered here, so the bracket is the limit of its values at nearby times and must vanish there as well. Therefore

\[\mu\ddot u+b\dot u+ku=F.\]
(2.4)

Each term has units of force: \(\mu\ddot u\) is mass times acceleration, \(b\dot u\) is a damping coefficient times velocity, and \(ku\) is a stiffness times displacement. Dividing by \(\mu\) gives the normalized form used later,

\[\ddot u+\frac{b}{\mu}\dot u+\frac{k}{\mu}u=\frac{F}{\mu}.\]

The same equation follows from Lagrange’s equations. They are worth writing once because they generalize directly to many coordinates. Let the Lagrangian be \(T-V\), and let \(D\) be the Rayleigh dissipation function. For a generalized coordinate \(u\),

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}-\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot u}=Q,\]
(2.5)

where \(Q\) is the generalized force. For this system \(L=\tfrac12\mu\dot u^2-\tfrac12ku^2\), \(D=\tfrac12b\dot u^2\), and \(Q=F\). The four derivatives are

\[\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}=\mu\dot u,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot u}=\mu\ddot u,\qquad \frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial u}=-ku,\qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot u}=b\dot u.\]

Substitution returns \(\mu\ddot u+ku+b\dot u=F\), which is (2.4). The energy route is a scalar identity; Lagrange’s route is the general rule that produces the same equation for any choice of coordinates. We use the energy route for intuition and Lagrange’s route for the matrix case, where the bookkeeping is easier.

2.3Many degrees of freedom

For the full structure, substitute (2.1) into the same balance. The stored energy is \(T+V\), and its rate of change is the power supplied by the external forces minus the dissipated power:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q+\tfrac12q^TKq\right)=\dot q^TBx-\dot q^TC\dot q.\]
(2.6)

Because \(M\) and \(K\) are constant and symmetric, the derivatives are

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\right)=\dot q^TM\ddot q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12q^TKq\right)=\dot q^TKq.\]

Substituting and collecting the terms on the left gives

\[\dot q^T\bigl(M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq-Bx\bigr)=0.\]

This must hold at every instant and for every admissible velocity \(\dot q\). Choose \(\dot q=e_i\), the vector with a one in position \(i\) and zeros elsewhere. The equation then says that component \(i\) of the bracket is zero. Doing this for each \(i\) makes every component zero, so the bracket itself is zero:

\[M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=Bx,\qquad y=Lq.\]
(2.7)

This is the equation that the rest of the page analyzes. The mass matrix multiplies acceleration, the damping matrix multiplies velocity, the stiffness matrix multiplies displacement, and the input matrix distributes the external forces. The sensor equation is algebraic: the measurement at each instant is a linear combination of the current displacements.

2.4What the matrices mean

Symmetry. Any square matrix can be split into a symmetric part and a skew-symmetric part. For a quadratic form, the skew part contributes nothing, because \(q^TAq=q^TA^Tq\) and the two terms cancel in the antisymmetric difference. Only the symmetric part matters in the energy. We therefore take \(M\), \(K\), and \(C\) symmetric without loss of generality. For \(M\) and \(K\), this follows from the energy itself. For \(C\), it is the viscous-damping assumption A2; damping mechanisms such as friction or gyroscopic effects are not described by a symmetric \(C\).

Positive definiteness. A symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive semidefinite if \(x^TAx\ge0\) for every vector \(x\), and positive definite if the inequality is strict whenever \(x\ne0\). The kinetic energy satisfies \(T\ge0\), and \(T=0\) only when every velocity is zero. That is exactly the statement that \(M\) is positive definite, \(M\succ0\). In particular, \(M\) is invertible. The strain energy satisfies \(V\ge0\); it is positive for every nonzero displacement only if the structure is restrained against rigid-body motion, in which case \(K\succ0\). A free-free structure has rigid-body motions with \(K\phi=0\), so \(K\) is only positive semidefinite. The dissipation satisfies \(D\ge0\), so \(C\succeq0\); it may be singular, meaning some motions are undamped.

Units. With \(q\) in metres and \(x\) in newtons, \(M\) has units of kilograms, \(C\) of newton-seconds per metre, and \(K\) of newtons per metre. Each term in (2.7) is a force. The input matrix \(B\) is dimensionless if \(x\) is a force; if an input is a torque or an acceleration, its units are absorbed into \(B\). The output matrix \(L\) carries whatever gain converts metres to sensor units.

A concrete case. Two masses \(m_1,m_2\) are connected by springs \(k_1,k_2,k_3\) and dampers \(c_1,c_2,c_3\), with \(k_1,c_1\) from the wall to \(m_1\), \(k_2,c_2\) between the masses, and \(k_3,c_3\) from \(m_2\) to the wall. Let \(q=[q_1,q_2]^T\). Then

\[M=\begin{bmatrix}m_1&0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}k_1+k_2&-k_2\\-k_2&k_2+k_3\end{bmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{bmatrix}c_1+c_2&-c_2\\-c_2&c_2+c_3\end{bmatrix}.\]
(2.8)

The off-diagonal entries are negative because the middle spring and damper pull the two masses toward each other: a positive displacement of \(m_1\) stretches the middle spring and produces a negative force on \(m_2\). The symmetry is visible. The quadratic forms are

\[V=\tfrac12\left[k_1q_1^2+k_2(q_2-q_1)^2+k_3q_2^2\right],\qquad D=\tfrac12\left[c_1\dot q_1^2+c_2(\dot q_2-\dot q_1)^2+c_3\dot q_2^2\right].\]

Each bracket is a sum of squares with positive coefficients, so \(V\ge0\) and \(D\ge0\). The matrix \(K\) is positive definite when \(k_1+k_2>0\) and the determinant \(k_1k_2+k_1k_3+k_2k_3>0\), which holds for positive springs. If a force acts on \(m_1\) only, \(B=[1,0]^T\). If both displacements are measured, \(L=I\). This example is small enough to check by hand and rich enough to show where the matrix entries come from.

2.5Lagrange’s equations for the matrix case

The same equation follows from Lagrange’s equations with \(q\) as the generalized coordinate. This is the derivation that makes the result independent of the particular coordinates chosen. With the Lagrangian \(T-V\) and generalized forces \(Q=Bx\),

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial (T-V)}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial\dot q}=Q.\]
(2.9)

Compute each term. Since \(T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\),

\[\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}=M\dot q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}=M\ddot q,\]

where the second equality uses the fact that \(M\) is constant. Since \(V=\tfrac12q^TKq\), component \(i\) of its gradient is

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i}=\frac12\sum_{j,k}K_{jk}\frac{\partial(q_jq_k)}{\partial q_i}=\frac12\sum_kK_{ik}q_k+\frac12\sum_jK_{ji}q_j=(Kq)_i,\]

where the last step uses symmetry, \(K_{ji}=K_{ij}\), and renames the summation index. The same calculation gives \(\partial D/\partial\dot q=C\dot q\). Substituting into (2.9), with \(\partial(T-V)/\partial q=-\partial V/\partial q\), gives

\[M\ddot q+Kq+C\dot q=Bx,\]

which is (2.7). Lagrange’s formulation is the reason the equation is valid for any set of generalized coordinates, not only for physical displacements. It also shows why \(M\) need not be diagonal: the mass matrix couples coordinates whenever the kinetic energy contains cross terms.

We now have the model. To find the motions the structure can sustain with no external force, set \(x=0\) and look for solutions that keep their spatial pattern while evolving in time.

3Modal analysis: free vibration

Free vibration is the structure’s motion when no force acts. Its solutions are the modes: special motions in which every coordinate oscillates at the same complex rate and keeps a fixed relative amplitude and phase. This section finds those motions, expresses them as real displacements, and assigns each one a natural frequency and a damping ratio.

3.1The exponential ansatz and the quadratic eigenproblem

Set \(x=0\) in (2.7):

\[M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=0.\]

This is a linear, constant-coefficient differential equation. Its solutions are built from exponentials \(e^{\lambda t}\), possibly multiplied by polynomials in \(t\) when eigenvalues repeat. We exclude repeats by A5, so the exponential alone is enough. Seek a solution in which the spatial pattern is fixed and only the scalar time factor changes:

\[q_c(t)=\phi e^{\lambda t},\qquad \dot q_c=\lambda\phi e^{\lambda t},\qquad \ddot q_c=\lambda^2\phi e^{\lambda t}.\]
(3.1)

The subscript \(c\) marks a complex auxiliary solution; the physical displacement is real and is recovered below. The vector \(\phi\ne0\) is the spatial pattern and \(\lambda\) is the complex rate, in inverse seconds. Substituting into the free equation gives

\[\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda C+K\right)\phi\,e^{\lambda t}=0.\]

The exponential is never zero, so it can be cancelled. Define the dynamic stiffness matrix

\[Z(s)=s^2M+sC+K,\qquad Z(\lambda)\phi=0,\qquad \det Z(\lambda)=0.\]
(3.2)

The last equality follows because a nonzero vector \(\phi\) is mapped to zero, so the matrix \(Z(\lambda)\) is singular. Conversely, if the determinant is zero, a nonzero null vector exists. Equation (3.2) is a quadratic eigenvalue problem: the eigenvalue appears squared, linearly, and not at all. Its characteristic polynomial \(\det Z(s)\) has degree \(2n\), because the leading term is \(\det(M)s^{2n}\) and \(M\) is invertible. It therefore has \(2n\) roots counted with multiplicity. Since the matrices are real, nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs.

For each root \(\lambda_m\), the corresponding \(\phi_m\) is a null vector of \(Z(\lambda_m)\). Its overall scale is arbitrary; multiplying \(\phi_m\) by a nonzero constant gives the same mode. For now, choose any convenient normalization.

A numerical solver rarely solves the quadratic problem directly. It writes the second-order equation as a first-order system in the state \(z=[q;\dot q]\), whose matrix is \(2n\times2n\). The eigenvalues of that matrix are the same \(\lambda_m\), and its eigenvectors stack \(\phi_m\) on top of \(\lambda_m\phi_m\). The equivalence is derived in Appendix A. The state form also justifies the exponential ansatz: the solution of \(\dot z=Az\) is a matrix exponential, and its eigenmodes are exponentials.

3.2Real motion from complex modes

The auxiliary solution \(q_c(t)=\phi e^{\lambda t}\) is complex, but the physical displacement is real. Because the system matrices are real, if \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(\phi\), then \(\bar\lambda\) is an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(\bar\phi\). A real solution is formed by combining the conjugate pair with conjugate coefficients. Let \(c\) be a complex constant set by the initial conditions, and let \(q_m(t)\) be the contribution of this pair:

\[q_m(t)=c\,\phi e^{\lambda t}+\bar c\,\bar\phi e^{\bar\lambda t}=2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(c\,\phi e^{\lambda t}\right).\]
(3.3)

The second equality is the identity \(z+\bar z=2\operatorname{Re}z\), applied entry by entry. The imaginary parts cancel in every coordinate, so each coordinate has a real displacement. Summing these contributions over the \(n\) conjugate pairs gives the physical free response; the \(2n\) real constants in the \(n\) complex coefficients \(c_m\) match the \(2n\) initial conditions on displacement and velocity.

Write the eigenvalue in terms of its real and imaginary parts, \(\lambda=\sigma+j\omega_d\), and apply Euler’s formula:

\[\lambda=\sigma+j\omega_d,\qquad e^{\lambda t}=e^{\sigma t}\left[\cos(\omega_d t)+j\sin(\omega_d t)\right].\]
(3.4)

If \(\sigma<0\), the envelope \(e^{\sigma t}\) decays. If \(\sigma>0\), it grows; a stable structure has \(\sigma<0\). A nonzero imaginary part produces oscillation. We choose the member of each conjugate pair with \(\omega_d>0\) and write it as \(\lambda=-\alpha+j\omega_d\) with \(\alpha=-\sigma>0\). The decay rate is \(\alpha\), in inverse seconds, and the damped angular frequency is \(\omega_d\), in radians per second.

When the damping matrix is zero, or is a linear combination of \(M\) and \(K\), the mode shapes can be chosen real and the coordinates move in phase or exactly out of phase. For general \(C\), \(\phi\) is complex: different coordinates reach their maxima at different times. The physical motion is still real after the conjugate pair is combined. The complex shape is a compact way to record those relative phases, not a complex displacement.

3.3The equivalent single-degree-of-freedom oscillator

We want to label each mode with a natural frequency and a damping ratio. Those labels come from a scalar oscillator, so first solve the scalar problem completely. Let a mass \(\mu>0\), spring \(k>0\), and damper \(b\ge0\) have displacement \(u(t)\). Set the force to zero in (2.4) and divide by \(\mu\):

\[\ddot u+\frac{b}{\mu}\dot u+\frac{k}{\mu}u=0.\]
(3.5)

First remove the damper by setting \(b=0\). The equation becomes \(\ddot u+(k/\mu)u=0\). A cosine at angular frequency \(\omega_n\) has second derivative \(-\omega_n^2\) times itself, so the equation is satisfied when \(\omega_n^2=k/\mu\). This is the undamped natural angular frequency, set by mass and stiffness alone. Its value in cycles per second is \(f_n=\omega_n/(2\pi)\).

Restore the damper and substitute \(u=e^{st}\). Cancelling the exponential gives the characteristic equation

\[\mu s^2+bs+k=0,\qquad s=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4\mu k}}{2\mu}.\]
(3.6)

The discriminant decides the type of motion. If \(b^2<4\mu k\), the roots are a complex conjugate pair and the response oscillates. If \(b^2>4\mu k\), both roots are real and negative, and the response is a sum of two decaying exponentials with no oscillation. At \(b^2=4\mu k\), the roots coincide; this is the boundary between the two behaviours. Call the boundary value the critical damping coefficient \(b_{\mathrm{crit}}\). Since \(b\ge0\),

\[b_{\mathrm{crit}}^2-4\mu k=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{\mu k},\qquad \zeta\equiv\frac{b}{b_{\mathrm{crit}}}=\frac{b}{2\sqrt{\mu k}}.\]

The symbol \(\equiv\) introduces a definition. The damping ratio \(\zeta\) is the actual damping divided by the critical value; it is dimensionless. A value \(\zeta=0.05\) means five percent of critical damping. The regimes are \(0<\zeta<1\) underdamped, \(\zeta=1\) critically damped, and \(\zeta>1\) overdamped. The factor of two in the denominator is not conventional decoration; it comes from the quadratic formula in (3.6).

Using \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/\mu}\), the coefficient \(b/\mu\) becomes \(2\zeta\omega_n\). Dividing (2.4) by \(\mu\) therefore gives the conventional form and its characteristic polynomial:

\[\ddot u+2\zeta\omega_n\dot u+\omega_n^2u=0,\qquad s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=0.\]

For \(\zeta<1\), the roots are \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). Comparing with a damped mode \(\lambda=-\alpha\pm j\omega_d\), the real parts match when \(\alpha=\zeta\omega_n\), and the imaginary parts match when \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\).

Now match the whole polynomial, not just the roots. The quadratic with roots \(\lambda=-\alpha+j\omega_d\) and \(\bar\lambda=-\alpha-j\omega_d\) and leading coefficient one is

\[(s-\lambda)(s-\bar\lambda)=(s+\alpha-j\omega_d)(s+\alpha+j\omega_d)=(s+\alpha)^2+\omega_d^2=s^2+2\alpha s+(\alpha^2+\omega_d^2).\]

Setting this equal to \(s^2+2\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}s+\omega_{n,m}^2\) and matching coefficients gives \(\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}=\alpha_m\) and \(\omega_{n,m}^2=\alpha_m^2+\omega_{d,m}^2\). Therefore

\[\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|=\sqrt{\alpha_m^2+\omega_{d,m}^2},\qquad \zeta_m=\frac{\alpha_m}{\omega_{n,m}},\qquad \omega_{d,m}=\omega_{n,m}\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}.\]
(3.7)

These are the modal parameters of mode \(m\). The decay rate \(\alpha_m\) says how fast its amplitude falls; the damped frequency \(\omega_{d,m}\) says how fast it oscillates; the damping ratio \(\zeta_m\) expresses the damping relative to critical. The natural frequency \(\omega_{n,m}\) is the modulus of the eigenvalue. It is the frequency the equivalent oscillator would have if its damping were removed while its modulus was held fixed, not necessarily the frequency obtained by removing damping from the whole structure. §3.5 explains that distinction, which matters for interpreting reported frequencies.

3.4Interactive: damping regimes

The formulas above are easiest to read as a picture. Release the oscillator from unit displacement with zero initial velocity: \(u(0)=1\), \(\dot u(0)=0\). The damping control ranges from zero to twice critical. At and above critical damping the poles are real and the response no longer oscillates; increasing the damping further makes the final return to equilibrium slower, because the slow pole approaches zero.

The plotted displacement solves the scalar oscillator with those initial conditions. For \(\zeta<1\), write \(\alpha=\zeta\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). The general underdamped solution is \(u=e^{-\alpha t}(C_1\cos\omega_dt+C_2\sin\omega_dt)\). The first initial condition gives \(C_1=1\). Differentiating, \(u(0)=-\alpha C_1+\omega_dC_2=0\), so \(C_2=\alpha/\omega_d\). For \(\zeta=1\), the general solution is \(u=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\omega_nt}\); the same two conditions give \(C_1=1\) and \(C_2=\omega_n\). For \(\zeta>1\), write \(\beta=\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\) and the real poles \(\lambda_1=-\alpha+\beta\), \(\lambda_2=-\alpha-\beta\). The solution is a sum of two exponentials; the two initial conditions give the coefficients below. In every case the displayed curve is the exact solution of the same equation with the same initial data:

\[u(t)=\begin{cases}e^{-\alpha t}\left[\cos(\omega_dt)+\dfrac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt)\right],&0\le\zeta<1,\\(1+\omega_nt)e^{-\omega_nt},&\zeta=1,\\\dfrac{-\lambda_2e^{\lambda_1t}+\lambda_1e^{\lambda_2t}}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2},&\zeta>1.\end{cases}\]
(3.8)

The middle expression is the limit of the underdamped expression as the two roots merge; the overdamped expression reduces to it in the same limit. The figure shows the poles on the left and the displacement on the right. The pole-plane scale adjusts to keep both roots visible.

3.5Two meanings of natural frequency

There are two different operations that both deserve the name “remove the damping”. For the equivalent scalar oscillator, we set its damping ratio to zero while keeping its frequency parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\). For the whole structure, we set \(C=0\) in (2.7) and solve a new eigenproblem. Let \(\Omega_r\) and \(\psi_r\) denote an undamped angular frequency and its displacement shape, with \(r\) indexing the undamped modes. Substituting a sinusoid into the undamped equations gives

\[M\ddot q+Kq=0,\qquad K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r.\]

For a single mass and spring, the two calculations agree: both give \(\sqrt{k/\mu}\). They also agree mode by mode when the damping is proportional, meaning \(C=aM+bK\) for constants \(a,b\); then the damped modes are the undamped modes with an added decay. For general damping, the damped modes can combine several undamped shapes, and \(|\lambda_m|\) need not equal any \(\Omega_r\). In this page, “natural frequency of mode \(m\)” always means the equivalent-oscillator parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\), computed from the damped eigenvalue. The undamped structural frequencies are a separate quantity. The distinction is derived in Appendix A.

The next part connects these free-vibration modes to a forced, measured response. The bridge is the transfer matrix, whose poles are exactly the eigenvalues found here.

Part IIFrom forces to spectra

4The transfer matrix and its residues

Free vibration identifies the rates and shapes the structure can sustain. To connect them to a measured response, we need to know how a force at one point produces motion at another. That relation is the transfer matrix. Its partial-fraction expansion separates the contributions of the individual modes, and the numerator of each term carries the mode shape.

4.1The Laplace transform

The Laplace transform turns a differential equation in time into an algebraic equation in a complex variable. For a signal \(q(t)\) defined for \(t\ge0\), define

\[Q(s)=\mathcal L\{q\}(s)=\int_0^\infty q(t)e^{-st}\,dt,\qquad s=\sigma+j\omega.\]
(4.1)

This is a Fourier transform of the exponentially weighted signal \(q(t)e^{-\sigma t}\) on the positive time axis. Choosing \(\sigma\) large enough makes the integral converge for any signal that grows no faster than an exponential. The set of such \(\sigma\) is the region of convergence. We work inside it and do not need its exact boundary.

The derivative rule follows from integration by parts. Let \(u=e^{-st}\) and \(dv=\dot q\,dt\), so \(du=-se^{-st}dt\) and \(v=q\):

\[\int_0^\infty\dot q(t)e^{-st}\,dt=\bigl[q(t)e^{-st}\bigr]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty q(t)e^{-st}\,dt=sQ(s)-q(0).\]

At the upper limit, \(q(t)e^{-\sigma t}\to0\) by the choice of \(\sigma\); at the lower limit, the term is \(-q(0)\). Applying the same rule to \(\dot q\) gives the second derivative:

\[\mathcal L\{\dot q\}(s)=sQ(s)-q(0),\qquad \mathcal L\{\ddot q\}(s)=s^2Q(s)-sq(0)-\dot q(0).\]
(4.2)

With zero initial displacement and velocity, each time derivative becomes multiplication by \(s\). We impose those initial conditions to isolate the response caused by the force; by linearity, the response to nonzero initial conditions can be added separately. The transfer matrix computed this way therefore describes the forced part of the response only, which is the part that matters for a structure in stationary operation.

The Laplace variable is complex, but the measured stationary response is described by its value on the imaginary axis, \(s=j\omega=j2\pi f\). The region of convergence of a stable system includes that axis. The Laplace transform is used to derive the algebraic relation; the Fourier transform is used to interpret it at a frequency.

4.2From the equation of motion to \(H(s)\)

Let \(X(s)\) and \(Y(s)\) be the Laplace transforms of the force vector \(x(t)\) and the measurement vector \(y(t)\), taken entry by entry. Apply the transform to (2.7) with zero initial conditions. Each term becomes algebraic:

\[\underbrace{(s^2M+sC+K)}_{Z(s)}Q(s)=BX(s),\qquad Y(s)=LQ(s).\]
(4.3)

The matrix \(Z(s)\) is the same dynamic stiffness that appeared in the free-vibration problem (3.2). Where it is invertible, solve the first equation for \(Q\) and substitute into the second:

\[Q(s)=Z(s)^{-1}BX(s),\qquad Y(s)=LZ(s)^{-1}BX(s).\]

The matrix multiplying \(X(s)\) is the transfer matrix:

\[Y(s)=H(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=LZ(s)^{-1}B.\]
(4.4)

The transfer matrix has size \(p\times r\). Its entry \(H_{ik}(s)\) maps input \(k\) to sensor \(i\). It is a matrix of rational functions of \(s\): the inverse \(Z(s)^{-1}\) has denominator \(\det Z(s)\), so every entry shares that denominator up to cancellation. The matrix \(Z(s)\) is singular exactly when \(s\) is an eigenvalue, so the poles of \(H\) are among the eigenvalues. A pole disappears from \(H\) only if the mode is unobservable at the sensors or unexcited by the inputs, or if a cancellation removes it from every entry. That possibility is examined after the residue is derived.

4.3Partial fractions at a simple pole

A rational function with simple poles can be written as a sum of one term per pole plus a remainder that is analytic at each pole. For one entry \(H_{ik}\), choose an eigenvalue \(\lambda_m\) and write

\[H_{ik}(s)=\frac{r_{ik,m}}{s-\lambda_m}+g_{ik,m}(s).\]
(4.5)

where \(g_{ik,m}\) collects every other term and is finite at \(s=\lambda_m\). This is an exact identity of the two functions, not an approximation. The coefficient \(r_{ik,m}\) is called the residue of \(H_{ik}\) at \(\lambda_m\). To isolate it, multiply by \(s-\lambda_m\) and let \(s\to\lambda_m\):

\[\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H_{ik}(s)=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}\left[r_{ik,m}+(s-\lambda_m)g_{ik,m}(s)\right]=r_{ik,m}.\]
(4.6)

The second term vanishes because a factor that goes to zero multiplies a function that stays finite. This is why the limit isolates the residue. Repeating the calculation for every sensor \(i\) and every input \(k\), and arranging the constants in their original positions, gives a constant matrix \(R_m\) and a remainder matrix \(H_{\mathrm{rest},m}\):

\[H(s)=\frac{R_m}{s-\lambda_m}+H_{\mathrm{rest},m}(s),\qquad R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s).\]

The matrix \(R_m\) has size \(p\times r\), with one coefficient for each sensor–input pair. It describes only the chosen pole. The remainder contains every other pole, including the conjugate pole \(\bar\lambda_m\). The assumption that the pole is simple, A5, is what makes the principal part a single term; a repeated pole would contribute terms with higher powers of \(1/(s-\lambda_m)\).

4.4Why the residue is rank one

The residue matrix is not an arbitrary matrix. To see its structure, separate the transfer matrix into the part that maps forces to all physical coordinates and the part that maps coordinates to sensors. Let

\[\mathcal T(s)=Z(s)^{-1}B,\qquad Q(s)=\mathcal T(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=L\mathcal T(s).\]

Let \(W_m\) be the residue of \(\mathcal T\) at \(\lambda_m\):

\[W_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)\mathcal T(s).\]

Multiply the identity \(Z(s)\mathcal T(s)=B\) by \(s-\lambda_m\) and take the limit. The left side becomes \(Z(\lambda_m)W_m\), because \(Z\) is continuous and the limit of the product is the product of the limits. The right side becomes zero, because \(s-\lambda_m\) multiplies the constant matrix \(B\):

\[Z(\lambda_m)W_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)B=0.\]
(4.7)

This equation holds column by column. Each column of \(W_m\) is a vector that \(Z(\lambda_m)\) maps to zero, so each column lies in the nullspace of \(Z(\lambda_m)\), the set of vectors mapped to zero. At a simple eigenvalue, that nullspace has dimension one. Therefore every column is a scalar multiple of the single null vector \(\phi_m\). If the scalar for input \(k\) is \(\gamma_{m,k}\), then column \(k\) is \(\phi_m\gamma_{m,k}\). Stacking the columns gives

\[W_m=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\gamma_{m,1}&\cdots&\phi_m\gamma_{m,r}\end{bmatrix}=\phi_m\gamma_m^T,\qquad \gamma_m^T=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{m,1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m,r}\end{bmatrix}.\]

Now apply the sensor matrix. Because \(L\) is constant, it passes through the limit:

\[R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s)=L\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)\mathcal T(s)=LW_m=(L\phi_m)\gamma_m^T=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(4.8)

This is the central structural result. The residue is an outer product of two vectors: the measured shape \(v_m\), of length \(p\), and the input-coupling vector \(\gamma_m\), of length \(r\). It has rank one, meaning that every column of \(R_m\) is a multiple of the same measured shape. The multiplier changes from input to input, but the spatial pattern does not. If \(v_m=0\), the sensors do not see the mode; if \(\gamma_m=0\), no modeled input excites it. Either way the residue is zero and the pole is absent from \(H\).

What rank one means here. For a fixed mode, every input produces the same sensor pattern, scaled by a complex number. Only the scalar \(\gamma_m^TX(s)\) changes with the input. The denominator \(s-\lambda_m\) divides every sensor equally, so it changes the frequency dependence but not the shape. This is why a single mode is visible as a single direction in a spectral matrix.

The input combination is worth writing explicitly. For two inputs with coupling coefficients \(2\) and \(-3\), and a measured shape \(v_m=[1,-2]^T\),

\[R_m=\begin{bmatrix}2v_m&-3v_m\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-3\\-4&6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&-3\end{bmatrix}.\]

Both columns retain the ratio \(1:-2\). Multiplying by an input vector combines them into \(v_m(2X_1-3X_2)\): one scalar excitation drives the whole pattern. The columns of the full transfer matrix need not be proportional, because the full matrix also contains the other modes. The rank-one statement is about one residue, not about \(H\) as a whole.

4.5The full modal sum

For real system matrices, the residue at the conjugate pole is the conjugate of the residue at \(\lambda_m\). The reason is that the coefficients of the rational function \(H(s)\) are real: if \(H(s)\) has a pole at \(\lambda_m\) with residue \(R_m\), then \(H(\bar s)=\overline{H(s)}\) has a pole at \(\bar\lambda_m\) with residue \(\bar R_m\). Summing over the \(n\) conjugate pairs, choosing the member with positive imaginary part in each pair, reconstructs the transfer matrix exactly:

\[H(s)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\frac{R_m}{s-\lambda_m}+\frac{\bar R_m}{s-\bar\lambda_m}\right),\qquad R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(4.9)

This is a full partial-fraction expansion, not an assumption that one mode dominates. It is valid under A5, including heavy damping, as long as the poles are simple. It also shows where a mode can disappear: a zero measured shape, a zero input coupling, or a cancellation in a particular entry. A mode that is absent from \(H\) is not absent from the structure; it is absent from this input–output pair.

Equation (4.9) is the bridge to measured data. The next section describes the random response whose second moments are built from \(H\), and then isolates one term of the sum.

5The response spectral matrix

In an operational measurement the forces are unknown and fluctuate from record to record. We cannot average the response itself, because its mean is zero and its instantaneous value depends on the particular force history. We average products of Fourier coefficients instead. That average is the spectral matrix, and it is the quantity FDD decomposes.

5.1From records to a spectral matrix

Let \(T\) be the duration of a record and let \(y(t)\) be the \(p\)-channel measurement on that record. Define its Fourier transform at frequency \(f\):

\[Y_T(f)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}y(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt,\qquad G_y(f)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb E\!\left[Y_T(f)Y_T(f)^H\right].\]
(5.1)

The vector \(Y_T(f)\) is random because the record is random. The expectation \(\mathbb E\) averages over all records that could have been generated under the same statistical conditions. The outer product \(Y_TY_T^H\) is a \(p\times p\) matrix; its \((i,k)\) entry is \(Y_i\bar Y_k\), the product of one channel with the conjugate of another. The factor \(1/T\) converts the squared transform, which has units of amplitude squared times time squared, into a density with units of amplitude squared per hertz. Integrating a diagonal entry over all frequencies gives that channel’s mean square, which equals its variance because the mean is zero.

The spectral matrix is two-sided: both positive and negative frequencies are included. For real signals, the negative-frequency part is the conjugate mirror of the positive-frequency part, so no information is lost by keeping only nonnegative frequencies. A one-sided spectrum, marked \(^+\), doubles the interior positive-frequency bins and keeps the DC and Nyquist bins unchanged. The conventions are collected in Appendix D.

The definition assumes that the expected product of two channels depends only on the time separation between the samples, not on the absolute time. That is wide-sense stationarity, A6. It guarantees that the limit in (5.1) exists and that the spectral matrix has a well-defined meaning. In practice the limit is replaced by an average over finite segments; ergodicity is the assumption that lets a time average estimate the ensemble average. The estimation details, including windowing and normalization, are in Appendix D.

5.2Hermitian positive semidefiniteness

The outer product has a symmetry that survives averaging. For any vector \(Y\),

\[\left(YY^H\right)^H=\left(Y^H\right)^HY^H=YY^H.\]

A matrix equal to its conjugate transpose is Hermitian. The expectation of a Hermitian matrix is Hermitian, so \(G_y\) is Hermitian. Its diagonal entries are real and nonnegative, because they are averages of \(|Y_i|^2\).

The matrix is also positive semidefinite. Choose any complex vector \(a\) of sensor weights. The scalar \(a^HY_T\) is a weighted combination of the Fourier coefficients, and its squared magnitude cannot be negative. Averaging preserves the inequality:

\[G_y^H=G_y,\qquad a^HG_ya=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb E\!\left[|a^HY_T|^2\right]\ge0.\]
(5.2)

These two properties are what FDD uses. The spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices says that \(G_y\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and real eigenvalues. Positive semidefiniteness says that those eigenvalues are nonnegative. Writing the eigenvectors as the columns of a unitary matrix \(U\) and the eigenvalues as the diagonal entries of a real diagonal matrix \(\Lambda\),

\[G_y=U\Lambda U^H,\qquad U^HU=I,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p),\quad \lambda_k\ge0.\]

For a positive-semidefinite Hermitian matrix, the singular values are the eigenvalues and the left singular vectors are the eigenvectors. The rank is the number of nonzero eigenvalues. A rank-one matrix has exactly one nonzero eigenvalue and one eigenvector; every other direction is in its nullspace.

5.3The input–output relation

The measured spectral matrix describes the response. To connect it to the mechanical model, use the transfer matrix. At a fixed frequency, the Fourier transform of the stationary output is the transfer matrix evaluated on the imaginary axis times the Fourier transform of the input:

\[Y_T(f)=H(j2\pi f)X_T(f).\]

This is the frequency-domain version of (4.4), with \(s=j2\pi f\). It holds for the forced part of the response under zero initial conditions; the initial-condition transient has decayed in stationary operation. Substitute into the outer product:

\[Y_TY_T^H=\left(HX_T\right)\left(HX_T\right)^H=HX_TX_T^HH^H.\]

At a fixed frequency, \(H\) is a constant matrix determined by the system; it does not vary from one realization to the next. It can therefore be taken outside the expectation. Applying the definition (5.1) to both sides gives the exact relation

\[G_y(f)=H(j2\pi f)\,G_x(f)\,H(j2\pi f)^H.\]
(5.3)

The input spectral matrix \(G_x\) has size \(r\times r\) and is Hermitian positive semidefinite by the same argument as \(G_y\). The output matrix has size \(p\times p\). Equation (5.3) separates the two things that determine the measurement: the force spectrum \(G_x\), which describes how the structure is excited, and the transfer matrix \(H\), which describes how it responds and what the sensors see. The next section substitutes the modal expansion of \(H\) into this relation.

5.4Conventions and estimation

Two conventions are easy to mix up, so they are stated once here and derived in Appendix D. The first is the conjugation order of the cross-spectrum. This page defines entry \((i,k)\) as \(Y_i\bar Y_k\). Some software defines it as \(\bar Y_iY_k\), which is the transpose and conjugate of this matrix. The two conventions describe the same physics, but the estimated shape is conjugated in the second. They must not be mixed within one calculation.

The second is the one-sided scaling. A one-sided spectral matrix keeps only \(f\ge0\) and doubles the interior bins, so that integrating it over \(f\ge0\) gives the same mean square as integrating the two-sided matrix over all \(f\). The DC and Nyquist bins are not doubled, because they have no negative-frequency partner. The Welch estimator that produces \(\widehat G_y\) from finite segments, its normalization, and the resolution–variance tradeoff are in Appendix D.

With (5.3) and the modal sum (4.9), the response matrix is an exact sum of rank-one modal terms. The next part asks when one of those terms dominates strongly enough that the leading eigenvector of \(G_y\) can be read as a mode shape.

Part IIIFinding modes in data

6When one mode dominates

The response spectral matrix is an exact sum of rank-one modal terms, but the measured matrix contains all of them at once. FDD becomes possible when one term is large enough that the others can be treated as a perturbation. This section isolates one term, expands the matrix exactly around it, and states the condition under which the expansion is dominated by a single measured shape.

6.1Isolating one pole

Choose a mode \(m\) and evaluate the transfer matrix on the imaginary axis, \(s=j\omega\) with \(\omega=2\pi f\). Separate the term for \(\lambda_m\) from every other term:

\[H(j\omega)=\frac{R_m}{j\omega-\lambda_m}+E_m(j\omega),\qquad R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(6.1)

The matrix \(E_m\) contains the conjugate pole and every other mode. It is finite at \(\omega=\omega_{d,m}\), because the other poles are at different complex frequencies (A5). The selected denominator is

\[j\omega-\lambda_m=j\omega-(-\alpha_m+j\omega_{d,m})=\alpha_m+j(\omega-\omega_{d,m}),\qquad |j\omega-\lambda_m|^2=\alpha_m^2+(\omega-\omega_{d,m})^2.\]

At \(\omega=\omega_{d,m}\), the denominator has magnitude \(\alpha_m\). The selected term therefore has magnitude proportional to \(1/\alpha_m\), which is large when the damping is small. The remainder \(E_m\) does not have that denominator and remains finite. This is the origin of the resonance peak: the numerator does not vanish, but the denominator becomes small.

6.2The exact four-term expansion

Substitute (6.1) into the input–output relation (5.3). Write \(P=R_m/(j\omega-\lambda_m)\) for the selected term and \(E=E_m(j\omega)\) for the remainder. Then

\[G_y=(P+E)G_x(P+E)^H=PG_xP^H+PG_xE^H+EG_xP^H+EG_xE^H.\]
(6.2)

This is exact. The first term contains the selected pole twice. The middle two terms mix the selected pole with the remainder, and the last term contains the remainder twice. Now evaluate the first term. Because \(P=R_m/(j\omega-\lambda_m)\) and \(P^H=R_m^H/(\overline{j\omega-\lambda_m})=R_m^H/(-j\omega-\bar\lambda_m)\), the denominator is \(|j\omega-\lambda_m|^2\):

\[PG_xP^H=\frac{R_mG_xR_m^H}{|j\omega-\lambda_m|^2}.\]

Substitute the factorization \(R_m=v_m\gamma_m^T\). Its conjugate transpose is \(R_m^H=\bar\gamma_mv_m^H\). Therefore

\[R_mG_xR_m^H=v_m\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m\,v_m^H=\left(\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m\right)v_mv_m^H.\]

The scalar in parentheses multiplies the outer product. Define the scalar spectral weight

\[G_y\approx d_m\,v_mv_m^H,\qquad d_m(f)=\frac{\gamma_m^TG_x(f)\bar\gamma_m}{\alpha_m^2+(2\pi f-\omega_{d,m})^2}\ge0.\]
(6.3)

The inequality holds because \(G_x\) is positive semidefinite. Since \((\bar\gamma_m)^H=\gamma_m^T\), the scalar is \(\gamma_m^TG_x\bar\gamma_m=(\bar\gamma_m)^HG_x(\bar\gamma_m)\), a quadratic form of a positive-semidefinite matrix, and cannot be negative. The frequency dependence is confined to the scalar \(d_m\). The direction \(v_m\) is fixed. If the force spectrum is approximately constant across the band, \(G_x\approx Q_x\), the weight becomes

\[d_m(f)=\frac{\gamma_m^TQ_x\bar\gamma_m}{\alpha_m^2+(2\pi f-\omega_{d,m})^2}.\]

This is a Lorentzian in frequency: it peaks at the damped frequency \(\omega_{d,m}\) and has half-width proportional to \(\alpha_m\). The numerator sets its height; the damping sets its width.

6.3The residual bound

The approximation in (6.3) consists of dropping the last three terms of (6.2). To say when that is legitimate, weight the selected term and the remainder by the excitation. Let \(G_x^{1/2}\) be the Hermitian positive-semidefinite square root of \(G_x\), and define

\[P_w=PG_x^{1/2},\qquad E_w=EG_x^{1/2}.\]

Then \(P_wP_w^H=PG_xP^H\), \(P_wE_w^H=PG_xE^H\), \(E_wP_w^H=EG_xP^H\), and \(E_wE_w^H=EG_xE^H\). The expansion becomes

\[G_y=P_wP_w^H+P_wE_w^H+E_wP_w^H+E_wE_w^H.\]

Let \(\|\cdot\|_2\) denote the matrix spectral norm, the largest amplification of a unit vector. The triangle inequality and the product bound \(\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2\) give

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le2\|P_w\|_2\|E_w\|_2+\|E_w\|_2^2.\]
(6.4)

If the weighted remainder is bounded by a fraction of the weighted selected term, \(\|E_w\|_2\le\varepsilon\|P_w\|_2\) with \(\varepsilon\ge0\), then

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)\|P_w\|_2^2.\]

The number \(\varepsilon\) is the relative size of the omitted response. It depends on the force spectrum, the modal visibility, and the separation of the poles, not on damping alone. A lightly damped mode can have a large \(\varepsilon\) if a nearby mode is strongly excited, and a heavily damped mode can have a small \(\varepsilon\) if it is the only mode in the band. The derivation of (6.4) is repeated in Appendix C with the same notation, so the two can be read side by side.

6.4What the assumption really says

The condition is not “the structure has one mode.” It has many. It is not “the damping is small.” Small damping makes the selected term taller and narrower, which helps, but it does not guarantee that the remainder is small. The condition is local in frequency: in this band, the selected mode’s contribution dominates the response that is actually excited and actually observed.

That distinction matters because a mode can be invisible for two different reasons. If \(v_m\) is small, the sensors are near a node of the mode and do not see it. If \(\gamma_m\) is small, the forces do not excite it. In either case the selected term is small even if the mode is lightly damped. Conversely, a strongly excited nearby mode can dominate a band even if the target mode is lightly damped. The bound (6.4) contains both effects through \(P_w\) and \(E_w\).

Three common failures follow. Close modes make \(E\) large, because the other pole is near the selected frequency. Colored forcing makes \(G_x\) vary across the band, so the weight is no longer a clean Lorentzian and the peak can shift. Correlated inputs change the scalar weight but do not by themselves destroy the rank-one structure of a single mode; they matter when several modes contribute. Measurement noise adds a full-rank term, which raises the smaller singular values and eventually destroys the numerical rank-one structure. These cases are revisited in §7.4 and §9.

6.5Worked example: two sensors

The abstract factorization is easiest to see in the smallest case. Two sensors record a structure in which only one mode contributes. Suppose that whenever the first sensor moves \(1\) mm in its positive direction, the second moves \(2\) mm in its negative direction. Let \(a(t)\) be the first displacement and \(y(t)\) the column vector of both measurements. The fixed vector \(v\) records the relative motion:

\[y(t)=\begin{bmatrix}a(t)\\-2a(t)\end{bmatrix}=v\,a(t),\qquad v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}.\]
(6.5)

The time dependence is entirely in \(a(t)\). It can be irregular; the spatial ratio remains \(1:-2\). In the frequency domain, let \(A(f)\) be the Fourier coefficient of \(a(t)\) over a record, and \(Y_1,Y_2\) the coefficients of the two sensors. Linearity gives \(Y_1=A\) and \(Y_2=-2A\). The products are

\[|Y_1|^2=|A|^2,\qquad |Y_2|^2=4|A|^2,\qquad Y_1\bar Y_2=-2|A|^2.\]

The separate power spectra preserve the amplitude ratio but lose the sign: squaring gives the same result for \(2A\) and \(-2A\). The cross product preserves their opposite motion. This is the concrete reason the cross-spectrum is retained. Averaging and normalizing gives the spectral matrix

\[G_y(f)=S_a(f)\,vv^T=S_a(f)\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\-2&4\end{bmatrix},\]
(6.6)

where \(S_a(f)\) is the PSD of the first signal. Where \(S_a>0\), the matrix has one independent column and rank one. Multiplication gives

\[G_yv=5S_a(f)\,v,\qquad G_y\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=0.\]
(6.7)

The eigenvalue depends on how strongly the motion is excited; the eigenvector gives the relative motion \(1:-2\) regardless of that strength. The null vector \([2,1]^T\) is orthogonal to \(v\), which is the one-dimensional nullspace of a rank-one Hermitian matrix. This is exactly the structure of (6.3): a scalar weight times an outer product, with the shape in the outer product.

Two caveats follow from the example. First, the shape is defined only up to scale: \([1,-2]^T\), \([2,-4]^T\), and \([-1,2]^T\) all describe the same relative motion, with the last differing by a phase of \(180^\circ\). Second, where \(S_a=0\), the matrix is zero and has no preferred direction; the shape is not defined at frequencies where the mode is not excited. FDD therefore estimates the shape at peaks, where the mode is present, not at every frequency.

7Frequency domain decomposition

FDD turns the rank-one approximation into a calculation that can be applied to a measured spectral matrix at every frequency. The calculation is an eigendecomposition. The interpretation of its leading vector as a mode shape is the approximation; the decomposition itself is exact.

7.1The SVD at each frequency

In practice \(G_y\) is replaced by an estimate \(\widehat G_y\) computed from finite records. The hat marks a quantity computed from data. At frequency \(f_i\), diagonalize the estimated Hermitian positive-semidefinite matrix:

\[\widehat G_y(f_i)=U_iS_iU_i^H=\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_k(f_i)\,u_k(f_i)u_k(f_i)^H,\qquad s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge s_p\ge0.\]
(7.1)

The columns of \(U_i\) are orthonormal eigenvectors, and \(S_i\) is diagonal with the eigenvalues on its diagonal. For a Hermitian positive-semidefinite matrix, the singular values are the eigenvalues and the left singular vectors are the eigenvectors; the singular value decomposition and the eigendecomposition coincide. The ordering \(s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\) is a convention; it makes the first term the largest.

If the exact matrix were rank one, only \(s_1\) would be nonzero. The estimated matrix is generally full rank because of other modes, noise, and finite-record errors. The question is not whether \(s_2\) is exactly zero, but whether it is small enough that \(u_1\) is close to the physical shape. That is a perturbation question, addressed in §7.2 and Appendix E.

7.2The leading singular vector

For the rank-one model \(G\approx d\,vv^H\) with \(v\ne0\), multiply by \(v\):

\[\left(d\,vv^H\right)v=d\,\|v\|^2\,v.\]
(7.2)

Thus \(v\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(d\|v\|^2\). Normalizing it gives the leading singular vector and value:

\[u_1\approx e^{j\theta}\frac{v}{\|v\|},\qquad s_1\approx d\,\|v\|^2.\]
(7.3)

The phase \(e^{j\theta}\) is arbitrary: if \(v\) is an eigenvector, so is \(e^{j\theta}v\) for any real \(\theta\). The normalization \(\|u_1\|=1\) fixes the magnitude but not the phase. Neither the phase nor the overall scale carries physical meaning in output-only analysis. Multiplying \(v\) by a complex constant and adjusting \(d\) by the inverse squared magnitude leaves \(d\,vv^H\) unchanged. This is why modal mass is not identifiable from output-only data.

For a perturbed matrix \(G=G_0+E\) with \(G_0=d\,vv^H\), the leading eigenvector is close to \(v/\|v\|\) when the perturbation is small relative to the gap between the first and second eigenvalues. The precise statement is a Davis–Kahan bound: if the leading eigenvalue is separated from the rest by a gap \(\delta\), then the sine of the angle between the estimated and true leading directions is at most \(\|E\|_2/\delta\). The derivation and the exact hypotheses are in Appendix E. The practical reading is that a large ratio \(s_1/s_2\) is evidence for a well-defined direction, and a ratio near one is evidence that the direction is not determined.

For the two-sensor example, (7.3) gives \(u_1=[1,-2]^T/\sqrt5\) and \(s_1=5S_a(f)\). The normalization is conventional; the ratio \(1:-2\) is the physical content.

7.3Peak frequency is not pole frequency

The peak of a measured spectrum is not exactly the damped frequency, and neither is exactly the undamped natural frequency. The distinction is visible even in a single-degree-of-freedom oscillator. With modal subscripts omitted, let \(h(j\omega)\) be the force-to-displacement transfer function with unit numerator,

\[h(j\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+j2\zeta\omega_n\omega}.\]

Its squared magnitude is

\[\left|h(j\omega)\right|^2=\frac{1}{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+4\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega^2}.\]
(7.4)

Under white forcing, the displacement PSD has the same frequency dependence up to a constant factor. Maximizing \(|h|^2\) is the same as minimizing the denominator \(D(\omega)=(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+4\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega^2\). Differentiate:

\[D'(\omega)=2(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)(-2\omega)+8\zeta^2\omega_n^2\omega=4\omega\left[\omega^2-\omega_n^2+2\zeta^2\omega_n^2\right].\]

The stationary points are \(\omega=0\) and the roots of the bracket, \(\omega^2=\omega_n^2(1-2\zeta^2)\). The bracket is negative just above zero and positive for large \(\omega\) when \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2\), so the positive root is a minimum of \(D\) and therefore a maximum of \(|h|^2\). When \(\zeta\ge1/\sqrt2\), the bracket is nonnegative for all \(\omega>0\), so \(D\) is increasing and the maximum is at \(\omega=0\). Therefore

\[\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}\quad(\zeta<1/\sqrt2),\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=0\quad(\zeta\ge1/\sqrt2).\]
(7.5)

For light damping, \(\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}\), \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\), and \(\omega_n\) are close, which is why peak picking is a useful first approximation. They are not equal. For \(0<\zeta<1/\sqrt2\), the ordering is \(\omega_{\mathrm{peak}}<\omega_d<\omega_n\). Colored forcing, acceleration measurements, and finite spectral resolution can shift the observed maximum further. An acceleration PSD carries an extra factor \(\omega^4\) relative to displacement, so its peak is biased upward; this is one reason to state the measured quantity before reporting a frequency.

7.4Two modes in one band

When two modes contribute in the same band, the spectral matrix is a sum of two rank-one terms:

\[G_y=d_1v_1v_1^H+d_2v_2v_2^H.\]
(7.6)

If the measured shapes are orthonormal, \(v_1^Hv_2=0\), the eigenvectors are exactly \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), with eigenvalues \(d_1\|v_1\|^2\) and \(d_2\|v_2\|^2\). The larger eigenvalue identifies the stronger mode. If the shapes are not orthogonal, this simplification fails. Multiply by \(v_1\):

\[G_yv_1=d_1\|v_1\|^2v_1+d_2v_2\left(v_2^Hv_1\right).\]
(7.7)

The second term generally points away from \(v_1\), so \(v_1\) is not an eigenvector unless \(v_2^Hv_1=0\) or \(d_2=0\). The leading singular vector is therefore a mixture, not a physical shape. What survives is the subspace: if \(d_1,d_2>0\) and \(v_1,v_2\) are linearly independent, \(G_y\) maps every vector into the two-dimensional span of \(v_1,v_2\). Every eigenvector with a nonzero eigenvalue lies in that span. The first two singular vectors recover the span, but not the individual shapes. Recovering each shape requires more information, such as a physical model, additional references, or a separation in frequency.

Orthogonal measured shapes are a special case, and they should not be confused with mass-orthogonal physical modes. A physical mode pair can satisfy \(\phi_1^HM\phi_2=0\) while the measured shapes satisfy \(v_1^Hv_2\ne0\); the sensor matrix \(L\) does not preserve the mass inner product. Equal singular values are a second special case: any orthonormal basis of the corresponding subspace is then an eigenbasis, so the decomposition does not determine a unique shape at all.

The modal assurance criterion (MAC) measures agreement between two nonzero vectors:

\[\operatorname{MAC}(u,v)=\frac{|u^Hv|^2}{(u^Hu)(v^Hv)}\in[0,1].\]
(7.8)

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality bounds \(|u^Hv|\) by \(\|u\|\|v\|\), so the ratio lies between zero and one. It is one for proportional vectors and zero for orthogonal vectors, and a common complex scale has no effect. FDD uses MAC to decide whether the direction at a neighboring frequency is the same mode or a different one.

7.5The algorithm

The derivation is now complete enough to state the procedure. The box is a summary, not a replacement for the assumptions above.

FDD, step by step

  1. Acquire. Record \(p\) synchronized channels with known units and orientations. Choose a segment length, a window, and an overlap.
  2. Estimate. Compute the cross-spectral matrix \(\widehat G_y(f_i)\) at each frequency bin, using the same segments and window for every channel pair. Use enough independent segments that the estimate is stable.
  3. Decompose. At each \(f_i\), compute the eigendecomposition (7.1). Store the singular values and the first few singular vectors.
  4. Find peaks. Plot \(s_1(f)\). A local maximum is a candidate resonance.
  5. Read the shape. At a peak, take \(u_1\) as the shape estimate. Check that \(s_1/s_2\) is large and that \(u_1\) is stable across neighboring bins by MAC.
  6. Estimate damping, if needed. Select the spectral bell around the peak by MAC, transform it to a correlation, and fit its decay (§8).

Output. Damped frequency \(f_{d,m}\), measured shape \(u_1\) up to scale and phase, and, with EFDD, damping ratio \(\zeta_m\).

The dominant cost is the eigendecomposition at each frequency, \(O(N_fp^3)\) for \(N_f\) bins and \(p\) channels. Since \(p\) is usually small compared with the number of time samples, this is inexpensive. The spectral estimate itself is dominated by the fast Fourier transforms over segments, \(O(N\log N)\) per segment. The expensive part of a real study is not the linear algebra; it is choosing the sensors and the spectral settings so that the assumptions hold.

8Estimating damping: EFDD

FDD recovers a shape and a frequency from the spatial structure of the spectrum. It does not, by itself, recover damping, because the peak location depends only weakly on damping and the peak height depends on the unknown force amplitude. Enhanced FDD uses a different part of the same information: the width of the spectral peak. It selects the part of the spectrum that belongs to one mode, transforms it back into a correlation, and fits the decay of that correlation.

8.1Selecting the spectral bell

Start at a frequency near the peak, where the leading singular vector is taken as a reference shape \(\hat v_m\). At each neighboring frequency, compare the candidate singular vector \(u_k(f)\) with the reference using the MAC of (7.8). If the MAC is above a threshold, the bin belongs to the same mode and is kept; otherwise it is rejected. The selected set of bins is the spectral bell.

The threshold is a practical choice, not a universal constant. A value of \(0.8\) is a common starting point. It should be high enough to exclude a neighboring mode and low enough to include the flanks of the peak, where the signal is weaker and the estimate noisier. The direction can move between the first and second singular vectors when the singular values cross, so the tracking must follow the direction by MAC, not the vector index. After selection, set every unselected bin to zero and inspect the bell: a bump on one side, or a bell that is not smooth, is evidence that another mode or a harmonic is contaminating the selection.

The selected spectrum is one-sided. Denote it by \(b^+(f)\), with \(b^+[k]\) its value at frequency \(k\Delta f\). The superscript \(+\) marks the one-sided scaling described in Appendix D; the DC and Nyquist bins are not doubled, and the interior bins are.

8.2From the bell to a correlation decay

The inverse Fourier transform of a one-sided PSD is the autocorrelation of the corresponding response. This is the Wiener–Khinchin relation. For a real, even correlation, the inverse transform reduces to a cosine integral:

\[\mathcal R_m(\tau)=\int_0^\infty b^+(f)\cos(2\pi f\tau)\,df\approx\Delta f\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}b^+[k]\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k\ell}{N}\right),\qquad \tau=\frac{\ell}{f_s}.\]
(8.1)

Here \(f_s\) is the sampling rate, \(N\) is an even transform length, \(\Delta f=f_s/N\) is the frequency spacing, and \(\ell\) is the lag index. The cosine appears because the negative-frequency half of a real spectrum is the conjugate mirror of the positive-frequency half; combining the two halves turns the complex exponential into a cosine. The discrete sum is a Riemann approximation to the integral; its accuracy depends on the frequency spacing and on the one-sided scaling.

For a single mode under the EFDD approximation, the correlation oscillates with a decaying envelope. Write the envelope as \(a_m(\tau)=A_0e^{-\alpha_m\tau}\), with \(A_0>0\) the extrapolated initial amplitude, and the damped period as \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_{d,m}\). The correlation is proportional to \(a_m(\tau)\cos(\omega_{d,m}\tau)\). The proportionality constant does not affect the decay rate.

8.3From log decrement to damping ratio

Sample the envelope at two times separated by \(k\) full periods, \(\tau\) and \(\tau+kT_d\). The ratio of the envelopes is \(e^{-\alpha_mkT_d}\), so its logarithm is \(-\alpha_mkT_d\). The logarithmic decrement \(\delta\) is defined as the logarithm of the amplitude ratio per period:

\[\delta=\frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{a_m(\tau)}{a_m(\tau+kT_d)}=\alpha_mT_d.\]
(8.2)

The exponential makes the ratio independent of the starting time \(\tau\); that is why any pair of corresponding peaks can be used. Substitute \(\alpha_m=\zeta_m\omega_{n,m}\) and \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_{d,m}=2\pi/(\omega_{n,m}\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2})\). The natural frequency cancels:

\[\delta=\frac{2\pi\zeta_m}{\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}}.\]

Solve for \(\zeta_m\). Square both sides, multiply by \(1-\zeta_m^2\), and collect terms:

\[\delta^2(1-\zeta_m^2)=4\pi^2\zeta_m^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\zeta_m^2=\frac{\delta^2}{4\pi^2+\delta^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\zeta_m=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}}.\]

The positive root is taken because \(\zeta_m\ge0\). Once \(\zeta_m\) is known, the damped frequency gives the natural frequency:

\[\zeta_m=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}},\qquad f_{n,m}=\frac{f_{d,m}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta_m^2}}.\]
(8.3)

In practice, fitting several peaks is better than using one ratio. Taking the logarithm of the envelope at peak times \(\tau_j\) gives \(\ln a_m(\tau_j)=\ln A_0-\alpha_m\tau_j\), which is a straight line in \(\tau_j\). A least-squares fit gives the slope \(-\alpha_m\), and hence \(\delta=\alpha_mT_d\). Same-sign peaks are one full period apart; alternating extrema are half a period apart, and using them without the correct spacing doubles or halves the apparent decrement. The fit should exclude the initial part, where the bell selection may distort the envelope, and the late part, where noise dominates.

8.4Practical bias

EFDD assumes that the bell contains one mode and that its correlation is a single decaying cosine. Several effects violate that assumption. A nearby mode produces beating, a slow modulation of the envelope that biases a single-exponential fit. Truncating the bell too narrowly removes part of the peak and biases the decay; truncating it too widely admits neighboring modes and noise. Spectral leakage, the spreading caused by a finite window, changes the shape of the bell and therefore the apparent decay. Nonstationary excitation changes the peak height during the record, which also distorts the envelope.

The defence is repetition. Compute the damping with several reasonable bell widths and fitting intervals and check that the result is stable. Compare the FDD and EFDD frequencies; they should be consistent with (3.7). If the estimate changes substantially with the truncation, the bell is not isolating one mode, and the estimate should not be reported as a single number without that caveat.

Part IVPractice

9Using the method on measurements

The calculation is short: estimate the cross-spectral matrix, decompose it at every frequency, inspect the dominant vectors around candidate peaks, and, if damping is needed, select a bell and fit its decay. The interpretation depends on a series of checks. This section collects them in the order in which they matter.

9.1Before the measurement

Sensor placement. A sensor at a node of a mode records none of that mode. Place sensors where the modes of interest are expected to have large motion, and include enough sensors to distinguish the shapes you care about. The measured shape \(v_m=L\phi_m\) can only be as informative as \(L\) allows.

Units and directions. Record the physical quantity (displacement, velocity, or acceleration), its units, and the positive direction of every channel. Arbitrary rescaling of one channel changes the geometry seen by the SVD and therefore changes the estimated shape. If channels are reoriented, apply the rotation before the spectral estimate, not after.

Synchronization. All channels must share a time base. A constant time offset between channels adds a frequency-dependent phase shift, which corrupts the cross-spectra and the shape. Check synchronization with a common excitation or a known signal before the test.

Duration and stationarity. The excitation must be stationary over the record, or at least over each segment used in the estimate. A record that contains a transient, a gust, or a change in operating condition violates A6 and produces a spectrum that is an average of different states.

9.2During spectral estimation

Same segments and window. Every entry of \(\widehat G_y\) must be computed from the same segments and the same window. Using different segments for different channel pairs destroys the phase relationships that the cross-spectrum is meant to preserve.

Enough averages. A single segment gives a noisy estimate whose rank can be artificially low. Averaging over independent segments reduces the variance. Overlap increases the number of segments but does not make them independent; the effective number of averages is smaller than the raw count.

Resolution. The segment length sets the frequency resolution. Zero-padding gives a denser frequency grid but does not improve the resolution, which is set by the segment duration and the window. A peak that is narrower than the resolution cannot be separated from a nearby peak.

Check the estimate. Plot \(s_1(f)\) and \(s_1/s_2\). A stable, well-separated peak with a large ratio is a candidate mode. A peak that moves when the segment length or the number of averages changes is not yet a stable estimate.

9.3Interpreting the decomposition

Stable direction. Across the bins of a peak, the leading vector should be stable by MAC. A direction that rotates rapidly with frequency is a mixture of modes, not one shape.

Rank-one check. A large \(s_1/s_2\) supports the rank-one interpretation. A ratio near one means that the second direction is comparable to the first; the leading vector is then not determined by the data alone.

Harmonic excitation. A sinusoidal force also produces a narrow, rank-one peak. The decomposition alone cannot distinguish a resonance from a forced harmonic. Check whether the frequency is stable across operating conditions, whether it coincides with a known forcing frequency, and whether the shape is consistent with a structural mode.

Mode visibility. An absent peak does not prove an absent mode. The mode may be weakly excited, or invisible at the available sensors. The same factors that make a residue small, \(v_m=0\) or \(\gamma_m=0\), make a mode absent from the measurement.

Nonlinearity. A nonlinear structure can produce peaks at harmonics and at combination frequencies, and its shapes can depend on amplitude. FDD assumes linearity; a shape that changes with excitation level is evidence against that assumption.

9.4Reporting

A result is reproducible only if the choices that produced it are reported. At a minimum, state the sensor coordinates and directions, the measured quantity and its units, the shape normalization and phase convention, the cross-spectrum convention, the segment length, window, overlap, number of averages, and frequency resolution. For EFDD, also report the selected bell, the MAC threshold, and the fitted lag interval. Report the frequencies as damped frequencies \(f_d\), or state explicitly that they are natural frequencies \(f_n\), because the two differ by the factor \(\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\).

The method is reliable when its assumptions are checked, not when they are assumed. The derivation in this page is meant to make those assumptions visible: the energy that produces the equation of motion, the simple poles that produce the rank-one residues, the stationarity that produces the spectral relation, and the dominance of one term that produces a single shape. When a check fails, the correct response is to report the mixture or the bias, not to treat the leading singular vector as a mode shape because the algorithm returned one.

AState-space linearization

The quadratic eigenvalue problem (3.2) can be written as an ordinary eigenproblem by including velocity in the state. Let \(z\) be the \(2n\)-component state vector and \(I\) the \(n\times n\) identity. With zero blocks of the same size, the free equation \(M\ddot q+C\dot q+Kq=0\) becomes

\[z=\begin{bmatrix}q\\\dot q\end{bmatrix},\qquad \dot z=Az,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\-M^{-1}K&-M^{-1}C\end{bmatrix}.\]
(A.1)

The upper block of \(\dot z=Az\) is \(\dot q=\dot q\), an identity. The lower block is \(\ddot q=-M^{-1}Kq-M^{-1}C\dot q\), which is the free equation multiplied by \(M^{-1}\). The matrix \(A\) is \(2n\times2n\), so it has \(2n\) eigenvalues counted with multiplicity. They are the same eigenvalues as the quadratic problem, as the next calculation shows.

Seek a state solution \(z=\Psi e^{\lambda t}\). Then \(\dot z=\lambda\Psi e^{\lambda t}\), and cancelling the exponential gives \(A\Psi=\lambda\Psi\). Write the upper and lower \(n\)-component blocks of \(\Psi\) as \(\Psi_1\) and \(\Psi_2\). The two block equations are

\[\lambda\Psi_1=\Psi_2,\qquad -M^{-1}K\Psi_1-M^{-1}C\Psi_2=\lambda\Psi_2.\]
(A.2)

The first equation gives \(\Psi_2=\lambda\Psi_1\). Substitute into the second and multiply by \(M\):

\[-K\Psi_1-\lambda C\Psi_1=\lambda^2M\Psi_1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda C+K\right)\Psi_1=0.\]

Thus \(\Psi_1\) is a null vector of \(Z(\lambda)\), so it is a mode shape \(\phi_m\), and the state eigenvector is

\[\Psi_m=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix},\qquad Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0.\]

Conversely, every nonzero solution of the quadratic problem produces a state eigenvector by stacking \(\phi_m\) and \(\lambda_m\phi_m\). The correspondence is exact:

\[Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix}=\lambda_m\begin{bmatrix}\phi_m\\\lambda_m\phi_m\end{bmatrix}.\]

In the quadratic problem, \(\lambda_m\) selects the matrix \(Z(\lambda_m)\) and \(\phi_m\) lies in its nullspace; \(\phi_m\) does not satisfy \(Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=\lambda_m\phi_m\). To recover a displacement mode shape from a state eigenvector, take its first \(n\) entries.

The two problems have the same \(2n\) eigenvalues, counting repeated roots according to multiplicity. Real matrices give conjugate pairs of nonreal roots. A defective eigenvalue has fewer independent eigenvectors than its multiplicity and requires polynomial factors in time; the simple-pole expansion used in this page excludes that case. The state form also explains why the exponential ansatz is sufficient: the solution of \(\dot z=Az\) is a matrix exponential, and the matrix exponential is a combination of \(e^{\lambda t}\) terms (with polynomial factors in the defective case).

A.1Damped modal parameters and undamped structural frequencies

There are two different meanings of “remove the damping.” For the equivalent scalar oscillator, we set its damping ratio to zero while keeping its frequency parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\). For the whole structure, we set \(C=0\) in (2.7) and solve a new eigenproblem. Let \(\Omega_r\) and \(\psi_r\) denote an undamped angular frequency and its displacement shape. Substituting \(q=\psi e^{j\Omega t}\) into the undamped equation gives

\[M\ddot q+Kq=0,\qquad K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r.\]
(A.3)

This is a generalized symmetric eigenproblem. The matrix \(M^{-1}K\) is not symmetric in the ordinary inner product, but it is symmetric in the \(M\)-inner product, and its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative when \(M\succ0\) and \(K\succeq0\). For a single mass and spring, (A.3) and the scalar oscillator both give \(\sqrt{k/\mu}\).

They also agree mode by mode under proportional damping, \(C=aM+bK\) with constants \(a,b\). To see this, apply \(Z(\lambda)\) to an undamped mode \(\psi_r\) and use \(K\psi_r=\Omega_r^2M\psi_r\):

\[Z(\lambda)\psi_r=\left(\lambda^2M+\lambda aM+\lambda bK+K\right)\psi_r=\left[\lambda^2+a\lambda+\Omega_r^2(1+b\lambda)\right]M\psi_r.\]
(A.4)

Since \(M\psi_r\ne0\), the bracket must vanish. It is a quadratic in \(\lambda\),

\[\lambda^2+(a+b\Omega_r^2)\lambda+\Omega_r^2=0.\]

Comparing with \(\lambda^2+2\zeta_r\Omega_r\lambda+\Omega_r^2\) gives \(\omega_{n,r}=\Omega_r\) and \(\zeta_r=(a/\Omega_r+b\Omega_r)/2\). The damped mode is the undamped mode with an added decay, and the two notions of natural frequency coincide. For general damping this is not true: the damped modes can combine several undamped shapes, and \(|\lambda_m|\) need not equal any \(\Omega_r\). In this page, “natural frequency of mode \(m\)” always means the equivalent-oscillator parameter \(\omega_{n,m}=|\lambda_m|\).

BThe residue formula via the adjugate

Section 4.4 shows that the residue is rank one, but it leaves the input-coupling vector \(\gamma_m\) in terms of a limit. This appendix evaluates that limit explicitly. The result is the formula used in implementations, and it shows exactly when a mode disappears from the transfer matrix.

The cofactor at row \(i\), column \(j\) of a square matrix is \((-1)^{i+j}\) times the determinant after deleting row \(i\) and column \(j\). The adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}Z\) is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. It remains defined at singular matrices and satisfies

\[Z\operatorname{adj}Z=\operatorname{adj}Z\,Z=(\det Z)I.\]
(B.1)

Wherever \(Z\) is invertible, \(Z^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}Z/\det Z\). The transfer matrix is therefore

\[H(s)=L\frac{\operatorname{adj}Z(s)}{\det Z(s)}B.\]

At a simple root \(\lambda_m\), the determinant has a simple zero and the adjugate has rank one. The rank statement follows from (B.1): at the root, \(\det Z=0\), so \(Z\operatorname{adj}Z=0\). Every column of the adjugate lies in the nullspace of \(Z(\lambda_m)\), which is one-dimensional, and the adjugate is not the zero matrix because the root is simple. Therefore

\[\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)=c_m\,\phi_m\ell_m^H,\]
(B.2)

where \(c_m\ne0\) is a constant, \(\phi_m\) is the right null vector, and \(\ell_m^H\) is the left null vector, satisfying \(Z(\lambda_m)\phi_m=0\) and \(\ell_m^HZ(\lambda_m)=0\). The two null vectors are nonzero because the root is simple.

Near the root, expand the determinant in a Taylor series. Let \(d'_m\) be its derivative at \(\lambda_m\). Then

\[\det Z(s)=(s-\lambda_m)d'_m+O\!\left((s-\lambda_m)^2\right).\]
(B.3)

The symbol \(O((s-\lambda_m)^2)\) denotes a remainder bounded by a constant times \(|s-\lambda_m|^2\) near the root. The derivative is given by Jacobi’s identity, which is valid also at singular matrices:

\[d'_m=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)Z'(\lambda_m)\right),\qquad Z'(s)=2sM+C.\]

Substituting (B.2) into the trace gives \(d'_m=c_m\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m\). Now form the residue limit. The factors \(s-\lambda_m\) cancel, as do the constants \(c_m\):

\[R_m=\lim_{s\to\lambda_m}(s-\lambda_m)H(s)=\frac{L\operatorname{adj}Z(\lambda_m)B}{d'_m}=\frac{(L\phi_m)(\ell_m^HB)}{\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m}=v_m\gamma_m^T.\]
(B.4)

Thus the input-coupling vector is

\[\gamma_m^T=\frac{\ell_m^HB}{\ell_m^H(2\lambda_mM+C)\phi_m}.\]

The denominator is nonzero at a simple root. If it were zero, the derivative of the determinant would vanish, and the root would not be simple. The residue vanishes precisely when the mode is unobserved, \(L\phi_m=0\), or unexcited, \(\ell_m^HB=0\). In a minimal state realization every state direction is reachable from the inputs and observable in the outputs, so no state pole is lost from the transfer matrix; individual entries can still omit modes.

For force-to-velocity and force-to-acceleration matrices, denote the transfer matrices by \(H_{\dot y}\) and \(H_{\ddot y}\). With zero initial conditions, differentiation becomes multiplication by \(s\), so \(H_{\dot y}=sH\). For acceleration, differentiating twice gives \(H_{\ddot y}=s^2H\). Its high-frequency limit is the feedthrough \(LM^{-1}B\); equivalently, \(H_{\ddot y}=LM^{-1}B+\tilde H(s)\) with \(\tilde H(s)\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\). The residues acquire factors \(\lambda_m\) and \(\lambda_m^2\), and the feedthrough adds a constant term that has no pole. Real or repeated roots require a different expansion; truncating the modal sum to a few modes leaves a residual.

Inverse expansion: [1], §3.5, equation (3.11). Factorized residues: [3], equations (2)–(3). The adjugate calculation supplies the intermediate steps.

CA bound on the omitted response

This appendix repeats the bound of §6.3 with the same notation, so that the main text and the proof can be read side by side. Assume the force spectral matrix \(G_x\) is constant across the band and denote it by \(Q_x\). Let \(Q_x^{1/2}\) be its Hermitian positive-semidefinite square root. Define the excitation-weighted selected term and remainder

\[P=\frac{v_m\gamma_m^T}{j\omega-\lambda_m},\qquad E=E_m(j\omega),\qquad P_w=PQ_x^{1/2},\qquad E_w=EQ_x^{1/2}.\]
(C.1)

Expanding (5.3) gives

\[G_y=P_wP_w^H+P_wE_w^H+E_wP_w^H+E_wE_w^H.\]

Let \(\|\cdot\|_2\) be the matrix spectral norm. The triangle inequality and the submultiplicative property \(\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2\) give

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le2\|P_w\|_2\|E_w\|_2+\|E_w\|_2^2.\]

If \(\|E_w\|_2\le\varepsilon\|P_w\|_2\), then

\[\left\|G_y-P_wP_w^H\right\|_2\le(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)\|P_w\|_2^2,\qquad \|P_w\|_2^2=\left\|P_wP_w^H\right\|_2.\]
(C.2)

The second equality says that the bound is relative to the size of the selected term itself, not to an unrelated scale. The number \(\varepsilon\) is a sufficient condition for an accurate rank-one approximation. It is not necessary: a matrix can be close to rank one even when this particular bound is loose. What the bound makes clear is the dependence on the excitation. A small \(\varepsilon\) requires the omitted response, weighted by the force spectrum, to be small compared with the selected response, also weighted by the force spectrum. Light damping alone does not produce that condition.

DSpectral conventions and estimation

The main text uses one convention for the cross-spectrum and one normalization for the spectral density. This appendix states both precisely, derives the normalization, and records the pitfalls that change a shape or a damping estimate.

D.1Conjugation order

Some sources define the cross-spectral matrix with \(\bar YY^T\) instead of \(YY^H\). Denote that matrix by \(\widetilde G_y\). Since \(Y^H=\bar Y^T\),

\[\widetilde G_y=\bar YY^T=G_y^T=\bar G_y.\]
(D.1)

Under this convention, the rank-one term becomes \(\widetilde G_y\approx d_m\bar v_mv_m^T\). The leading vector estimates \(\bar v_m\), so it must be conjugated to recover the shape convention used here. Both conventions are valid; mixing them within one calculation is not. SciPy’s csd(a, b) uses conjugate-first ordering. If y[i] contains channel \(i\), the entry \((i,j)\) of this page’s matrix is obtained with csd(y[j], y[i]). [6]

D.2Welch’s estimate

Welch’s method divides a record into possibly overlapping segments, applies the same window to every channel, and averages the spectral products. Detrending removes a fitted mean or trend. A window assigns a real weight to each sample to control the spectral spreading caused by a finite record.

Let \(N_b\) be the number of segments, \(N_w\) their length in samples, and \(w[\nu]\) the window at within-segment index \(\nu\). Indices \(b\) and \(k\) label segments and frequency bins. Write \(Y_b[k]\) for the vector of unnormalized discrete Fourier transform (DFT) coefficients after detrending and windowing. A fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the DFT efficiently. The one-sided factor \(\eta_k\) is \(2\) at interior positive-frequency bins and \(1\) at DC and Nyquist, when present. At sampling rate \(f_s\), the estimate is

\[\widehat G_y^+[k]=\frac{\eta_k}{N_b f_s\sum_{\nu=0}^{N_w-1}w[\nu]^2}\sum_{b=1}^{N_b}Y_b[k]Y_b[k]^H.\]
(D.2)

The normalization is easiest to check on white noise. Suppose a channel is white with variance \(\sigma^2\) and sampling rate \(f_s\). Its two-sided PSD is constant, \(G=\sigma^2/f_s\), because integrating a constant over the Nyquist band \([-f_s/2,f_s/2]\) gives \(Gf_s=\sigma^2\). For one segment, the expected squared magnitude of the windowed DFT coefficient is \(\mathbb E|Y_b[k]|^2=\sigma^2\sum_\nu w[\nu]^2\). Substituting \(G=\sigma^2/f_s\) gives

\[\mathbb E|Y_b[k]|^2=G\,f_s\sum_{\nu=0}^{N_w-1}w[\nu]^2.\]

Dividing by \(f_s\sum w^2\) therefore gives an unbiased estimate of \(G\), and averaging over \(N_b\) segments reduces its variance. The factor \(\eta_k\) converts the two-sided density to the one-sided convention. For a colored signal the window convolves the spectrum, so the estimate is a smoothed version of the true density; the normalization preserves the integral, not the exact shape.

Using identical segments and windows preserves the Hermitian positive-semidefinite structure. Each segment contributes at most one independent direction to the average, so the estimated rank cannot exceed \(N_b\). This is a finite-data rank, not a physical one. Averaging reduces variability between estimates, but it does not eliminate a persistent noise or forcing spectrum. Longer segments improve frequency resolution; more effectively independent segments improve averaging. Overlap does not make segments independent. Appending zeros before the DFT only samples the same windowed spectrum more densely; it does not improve the resolution set by the segment duration and window. [5]

D.3Density units and the inverse transform

The spectra here are densities per hertz. With superscripts \((f)\) and \((\omega)\) labeling density per hertz and per radian per second, the change of variables \(d\omega=2\pi\,df\) gives

\[G^{(\omega)}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}G^{(f)}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right).\]
(D.3)

For the EFDD inverse transform, the one-sided spectrum must be mirrored to negative frequencies before the inverse DFT. Halve the one-sided interior positive bins, copy them to the corresponding negative bins, and keep the DC and Nyquist endpoint weights from (D.2). With the inverse DFT normalization \(1/N\), the correlation is \(f_s\) times the inverse DFT of the mirrored spectrum, because the frequency spacing is \(\Delta f=f_s/N\) and the inverse transform sums \(\Delta f\,G[k]e^{j2\pi k\ell/N}\). Normalizing by \(\mathcal R_m(0)\) removes an overall scale but does not repair incorrect mirroring or incorrect endpoint weights. An incorrect one-sided scaling is a common cause of a biased damping estimate. [5]

D.4Additive sensor noise

Let \(\eta(t)\) denote sensor noise and \(G_\eta\) its PSD. If the noise and structural response have zero cross-covariance at every lag, their spectra add:

\[G_{y+\eta}=G_y+G_\eta.\]
(D.4)

Correlated noise introduces additional cross terms. A large singular value can therefore reflect the measurement process as well as the structure. Noise raises all singular values, but it affects the smaller ones proportionally more, so it reduces the ratio \(s_1/s_2\) that the rank-one interpretation relies on.

EA perturbation bound for the leading singular vector

Section 7.2 claims that the leading singular vector is close to the physical shape when the perturbation is small. This appendix makes that statement precise. The argument is short and gives the main dependence; a sharper version with the spectral gap is the Davis–Kahan theorem. [11]

Let \(G=G_0+E\) be Hermitian, with \(G_0=d\,vv^H\), \(\|v\|=1\), and \(d>0\). Let \(u\) be a unit eigenvector of \(G\) for its largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\), and assume \(\lambda_1>0\). Decompose \(u\) into a component along \(v\) and a component orthogonal to it:

\[u=\alpha v+\beta w,\qquad w\perp v,\qquad \|w\|=1,\qquad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.\]

The angle \(\theta\) between \(u\) and \(v\) satisfies \(\sin\theta=|\beta|\). Because \(u\) is an eigenvector, \(Gu=\lambda_1u\). Take the inner product with \(w\):

\[\lambda_1\beta=w^HGu=w^H(G_0+E)u.\]

The unperturbed term vanishes: \(w^HG_0u=d\,w^Hv(v^Hu)=0\), because \(w\perp v\). Therefore \(\lambda_1\beta=w^HEu\), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\[|\beta|\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1},\qquad \sin\theta\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1}.\]
(E.1)

This is the basic bound. It says that the angular error is controlled by the perturbation relative to the leading eigenvalue. Since \(\lambda_1\) is approximately \(d\), and \(s_1\) estimates \(\lambda_1\), a large \(s_1\) relative to the perturbation is favourable.

A sharper form replaces \(\lambda_1\) by the gap between the first and second eigenvalues. If the leading eigenvalue is simple and the perturbation does not close the gap, the Davis–Kahan theorem gives

\[\sin\theta\le\frac{\|E\|_2}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}.\]

When the second eigenvalue is close to the first, the denominator is small and the bound is weak, which is the quantitative version of the statement that a ratio \(s_1/s_2\) near one leaves the direction undetermined. When the gap is large, the direction is stable even if the absolute perturbation is not tiny. The bound is sufficient, not necessary: a direction can be stable for reasons this inequality does not capture.
